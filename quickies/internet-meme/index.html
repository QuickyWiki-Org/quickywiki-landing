Internet Meme

Richard Dawkins coined "meme" in 1976 for a unit of culture that spreads like a gene. The internet took the word — and the newest ones are being called brain rot and AI slop.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 13 terms decoded

A Biologist's Word, Borrowed

An Internet meme is a cultural item — an idea, behaviour or style — that spreads across the Internet, now primarily through social media.

The word did not begin online. Richard Dawkins introduced meme in his 1976 book *The Selfish Gene*, to describe a conceptual unit of cultural transmission, analogous to — and deliberately sounding like — the gene in biology. His point was that ideas replicate, mutate and compete for survival in much the way genes do.

The internet-specific term came later. Mike Godwin coined Internet meme in 1993, describing how memes proliferated through early online communities: message boards, Usenet groups and email. A concept invented to explain culture in general turned out to describe one specific network with uncanny precision.

Why They Behave the Way They Do

Memes manifest in many formats — images, videos including GIFs, and other viral content. But the recognisable behaviour comes from four key characteristics:

- A tendency to be parodied
- Use of intertextuality, referencing other works and other memes
- Viral dissemination
- Continual evolution

That last pair explains why memes feel alive in a way most media does not. A film is finished when it is released; a meme is a starting position. Each person who reposts it can alter it, and the alterations that get copied are the ones that survive — which is precisely the mechanism Dawkins was describing.

Intertextuality is also why memes are so hard to explain to anyone outside the context. A meme frequently means nothing on its own, because its meaning lives in what it is quoting.

From Message Boards to TikTok

The platforms shaped the form. The emergence of YouTube, Twitter, Facebook and Instagram further diversified memes and accelerated their spread.

Newer genres followed the newer platforms. Dank and surrealist memes are among the more recent, alongside formats like short-form video popularised by Vine and TikTok.

And the vocabulary has turned notably sour. Newer internet memes — specifically those of low quality — are often classified as brain rot or AI slop. That is a striking development for a medium barely thirty years old: it has generated its own internal terms of contempt, distinguishing memes made by people who mean something from content generated in bulk to farm attention.

Studied, Monetised, Litigated

Memes are now recognised as a significant aspect of Internet culture and are the subject of academic research — no longer only a thing that happens, but a thing that is formally studied.

Their reach across serious domains is broader than their frivolous reputation suggests. They appear across marketing, economics, finance, politics, social movements, religion and healthcare — carrying arguments in fields where the stakes are money, belief and public health.

There is a legal edge too. Memes are often viewed as falling under fair use protection, since parody and commentary are typically protected. But their incorporation of material from pre-existing works can still result in copyright disputes — the same intertextuality that makes a meme legible is what puts it in reach of the law.

10 key facts

  1. Richard Dawkins introduced the word meme in his 1976 book The Selfish Gene, as a cultural equivalent of the gene.
  2. The term Internet meme was coined separately by Mike Godwin in 1993.
  3. Early internet memes spread through message boards, Usenet groups and email before social media existed.
  4. Key characteristics of memes include parody, intertextuality, viral spread and continual evolution.
  5. A meme frequently means nothing on its own because its meaning depends on what it references.
  6. Dank and surrealist memes are among the newer genres, alongside short-form video popularised by Vine and TikTok.

  8. Memes are now the subject of academic research and recognised as a significant part of Internet culture.

Key terms, decoded

AI slop
Bulk-generated AI content made to attract attention rather than to be good.
brain rot
A dismissive term for low-quality online content thought to dull the people consuming it.
cultural transmission
The passing of ideas, behaviours and customs from one person or generation to another.
fair use
A legal principle allowing limited reuse of copyrighted material for purposes such as comment or parody.
gene
The unit of biological inheritance, which Dawkins used as the model for how ideas replicate.
GIF
A simple image format that supports short looping animations without sound.
intertextuality
The way a work draws meaning from referencing other works the audience already knows.
meme
A unit of culture, such as an idea or style, that spreads from person to person and changes as it goes.
message board
An online forum where users post messages in threaded discussions.
parody
An imitation of something, exaggerated for comic or critical effect.
short-form video
Very brief video clips, typically under a minute, designed for rapid scrolling feeds.
Usenet
An early worldwide discussion system organised into topic groups, predating the modern web.
viral
Spreading rapidly from person to person online, in the manner of an infection.
