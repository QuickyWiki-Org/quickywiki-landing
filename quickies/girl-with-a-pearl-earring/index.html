Girl with a Pearl Earring

She's one of the most recognizable faces in art history, but Vermeer never gave her a name — and there's a real chance her famous pearl isn't a pearl at all.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 6 terms decoded

A Face Nobody Can Name

Nobody knows who she was. Vermeer painted her around 1665 and never recorded her name anywhere. For centuries, she's simply been "the girl," a mystery hiding in plain sight inside one of the world's most recognizable paintings.

Technically, she isn't even a portrait. Vermeer painted a tronie, the Dutch term for a study of a head not meant to depict a specific, real person. He dressed his model in an exotic turban and let her gaze linger over her shoulder, aiming for mood and character rather than capturing an actual sitter's likeness.

That hasn't stopped the guessing game. Some believed she was Vermeer's own daughter, though historians now dismiss that idea. Art historian Andrew Graham-Dixon has argued she's Magdalena, the 12-year-old daughter of Vermeer's wealthiest patron. Others think she isn't a real girl at all, but a biblical figure or a mythological Sibyl.

The Pearl That Might Be Fake

The single most famous accessory in art history might not even exist. In 2014, Dutch astrophysicist Vincent Icke made a startling claim: the "pearl" dangling from the girl's ear is probably not a pearl at all, but a piece of polished tin.

Icke's case rests on physics. He pointed to the earring's shape, its unusually large size, and the way light bounces off it — its specular reflection — arguing that real pearls simply don't glint that way. Later researchers noticed something even stranger: the earring has no outline and no hook connecting it to her earlobe. It's really just a bright highlight, floating in space.

Sold for Less Than a Cup of Coffee

This masterpiece once sold for pocket change. In 1881, a Dutch collector named Arnoldus Andries des Tombe bought it at a Hague auction for two guilders plus thirty cents in buyer's premium — a sum worth roughly €24 in 2015 money. At the time, nobody thought they were buying a Vermeer masterpiece.

The painting wasn't even in decent shape; parts of the paint layer had already come loose. Des Tombe had no heirs, so when he died, he left the painting and several others to the Mauritshuis museum in The Hague in 1902. It has called that museum home ever since.

Secrets the Scientists Found

The painting has given up its secrets slowly. A 1994 restoration revealed that the now-mottled dark background wasn't always so drab: Vermeer originally painted it a deep, enamel-like green, achieved by layering a thin transparent glaze over black paint. Sadly, two of his organic pigments, indigo and weld, have faded badly over the centuries.

A 2018 research project called "The Girl in the Spotlight" used non-invasive scanning to dig even deeper. Scientists found delicate eyelashes invisible to the naked eye, a painted green curtain hidden behind her head, and evidence that Vermeer changed his composition as he worked. The findings confirmed something touching: he wasn't idealizing a face from imagination. He was painting a real person, in a real space.

Hidden From the Nazis, Attacked With Soup

During the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands, the painting went into hiding. It spent two years sealed inside a bombproof bunker underneath the Mauritshuis, then was moved to the St. Pietersberg caves near Maastricht for safekeeping until the Netherlands was liberated in May 1945. Only afterward did it return to public view.

Its fame keeps drawing attention, not all of it welcome. In October 2022, a Just Stop Oil activist tried to glue his head to the protective glass while another protester covered him in tomato soup. The painting was undamaged, and three people were arrested. It's also inspired plenty of tributes, including a 2014 Banksy mural swapping the pearl for a screeching alarm box.

Key terms, decoded

buyer's premium
An extra fee added on top of the winning bid at an auction, paid by the buyer in addition to the sale price.
glaze
A thin, see-through layer of paint brushed over another layer to change its color or add depth, a technique painters have used for centuries.
indigo
A deep blue-purple pigment originally made from plants, used by artists for centuries to color paint and dye fabric.
specular reflection
The bright, mirror-like highlight created when light bounces directly off a shiny surface, instead of scattering softly across a duller one.
tronie
A Dutch word from the 1600s for a painted study of a face or expression, not meant to be a portrait of a specific named person.
weld
A yellow pigment made from a flowering plant, historically used by painters to create bright yellow and green tones.
