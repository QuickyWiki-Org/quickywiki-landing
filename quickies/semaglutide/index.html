Semaglutide

It was made for type 2 diabetes. It became one of the most prescribed drugs in America — more than 25 million prescriptions in a single year — for something else entirely.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 10 terms decoded

One Molecule, Many Names

Semaglutide is an anti-diabetic medication used to treat type 2 diabetes, and an anti-obesity medication used for weight management. It can be given by subcutaneous injection or taken orally.

The naming is genuinely confusing, because the same molecule is sold under different brands for different uses. Its originator Novo Nordisk sells it worldwide as Ozempic and Rybelsus for diabetes, and as Wegovy for weight management. Under the brand names Wegovy and Kayshild it is also used to treat metabolic-associated steatohepatitis and liver cirrhosis, and Wegovy is additionally used to reduce the risk of heart attacks.

It is also sold as a generic drug by several companies in the Indian and Canadian markets. So the drug people argue about under one famous brand name is, chemically, the same substance appearing in several very different conversations at once.

Imitating a Gut Hormone

Semaglutide is a peptide similar to the hormone glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), modified with a side chain, and it acts as a GLP-1 receptor agonist.

An agonist is a substance that activates a receptor the way the body's own signalling molecule would. So rather than introducing a foreign mechanism, semaglutide impersonates a hormone the body already uses — one involved in regulating blood sugar and appetite.

That design explains both the intended effect and the side effects. The most common are nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and constipation — a cluster concentrated in the digestive system, which is where the hormone it mimics does much of its work. The side chain modification is what allows it to persist in the body long enough to be practical as a medication.

Approved Quietly, Then Everywhere

Semaglutide's regulatory history looks unremarkable until you set it beside its later fame.

It was approved for medical use in the United States in 2017, then in Canada, the European Union and Japan in 2018, and in Australia in 2019.

Those approvals passed without much public attention. What followed did not. In 2023, semaglutide was the nineteenth most commonly prescribed medication in the United States, with more than 25 million prescriptions. A drug approved for diabetes six years earlier had become one of the most prescribed medicines in the country — a scale of adoption that pharmaceutical products almost never reach that quickly, and one driven largely by a use that was not its original headline indication.

On the Essential Medicines List

Semaglutide appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines — the set of medicines the WHO considers necessary for a basic functioning health system.

That listing is a meaningful marker. It is not a popularity ranking; it signals that a medicine is judged to meet priority health needs, taking efficacy, safety and comparative cost into account.

Its range of approved uses keeps widening in a way that is unusual for a single molecule: type 2 diabetes, weight management, metabolic-associated steatohepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and reducing heart attack risk. Each of those is a distinct patient population with a distinct clinical problem. The reason one drug reaches so many is that it works on a hormonal signalling pathway which sits upstream of several conditions at once — which is also why its story has become as much about supply, demand and access as about pharmacology.

10 key facts

  1. Semaglutide is the same molecule sold as Ozempic and Rybelsus for diabetes and as Wegovy for weight management.
  2. It works by imitating GLP-1, a hormone the body already uses to help regulate blood sugar and appetite.
  3. It can be given either by injection under the skin or taken by mouth.
  4. The most common side effects are digestive: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain and constipation.
  5. It was approved in the United States in 2017, in Canada, the EU and Japan in 2018, and in Australia in 2019.
  6. In 2023 it was the nineteenth most commonly prescribed medication in the United States.
  7. More than 25 million prescriptions for it were written in the United States in a single year.
  8. It appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
  9. Under the names Wegovy and Kayshild it is also used for metabolic-associated steatohepatitis and liver cirrhosis.
  10. It is sold as a generic drug by several companies in the Indian and Canadian markets.

Key terms, decoded

cirrhosis
Severe scarring of the liver that stops it working properly.
generic drug
A medicine sold without a brand name after the original patent expires, containing the same active substance.
GLP-1
A gut hormone released after eating that helps control blood sugar and signals fullness.
hormone
A chemical messenger released into the blood that changes how other parts of the body behave.
List of Essential Medicines
The World Health Organization's list of medicines judged necessary for a basic health system.
peptide
A short chain of amino acids, smaller than a full protein.
receptor agonist
A substance that switches on a cell receptor in the same way the body's own signalling molecule would.
steatohepatitis
Inflammation of the liver associated with a build-up of fat in its cells.
subcutaneous injection
An injection given into the fatty layer just under the skin rather than into a vein or muscle.
type 2 diabetes
A long-term condition in which the body cannot use insulin properly, so blood sugar stays too high.
