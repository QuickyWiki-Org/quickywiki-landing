Smartphone

It replaced the camera, the map, the music player, the calculator and the phone book. By 2020, three quarters of everyone alive was carrying one.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 14 terms decoded

The Devices It Quietly Killed

A smartphone is a mobile device that combines the functions of a traditional feature phone with advanced computing. Touchscreen, apps, web, email, messaging, media — all in one object.

The more revealing way to define it is by what it absorbed. Smartphones have largely replaced personal digital assistants, handheld and palm-sized PCs, portable media players, point-and-shoot cameras, camcorders and GPS tracking units — and, to a lesser extent, handheld games consoles, e-readers and pocket calculators.

That is an entire shelf of consumer electronics collapsed into one slab of glass. Each of those was a separate purchase, a separate thing to carry, a separate industry. What separates a smartphone from a feature phone is the depth underneath: more advanced hardware, extensive mobile operating systems, internet access, business applications, mobile payments, and multimedia across music, video, gaming, radio and television.

A Pocket Full of Sensors

The part most people never think about is how much a phone is measuring while it sits in a pocket.

Smartphones typically contain metal–oxide–semiconductor integrated circuits alongside a set of sensors: accelerometers, barometers, gyroscopes and magnetometers. Both pre-installed and third-party software can use them.

So the device knows which way it is facing, how it is tilted, how it is moving, and the air pressure around it. That is what lets a map rotate as you turn, a step counter work without a wristband, and a screen flip when you lie down. On the communication side it supports LTE, 5G NR, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and satellite navigation — and by the mid-2020s manufacturers began integrating satellite messaging and emergency services, extending coverage into remote areas with no cellular signal at all.

Why They All Look the Same

Phone design converged on a single shape, and there is a specific moment it happened.

Following the rising popularity of the iPhone in the late 2000s, the majority of smartphones adopted thin, slate-like form factors with large capacitive touch screens supporting multi-touch gestures — rather than physical keyboards.

That was not an obvious outcome. Physical keyboards were the premium feature of the era, and giving them up in favour of a glass rectangle was a real bet. It won so completely that the alternative essentially vanished. Most modern smartphones also let users download or buy additional applications from a centralised app store, and commonly support cloud storage, cloud synchronisation and virtual assistants.

Three Quarters of Humanity

The adoption numbers are difficult to match against any other technology.

As of 2014, over a billion smartphones were being sold globally every year. In 2019 alone, 1.54 billion units shipped worldwide. And as of 2020, 75.05% of the world population were smartphone users.

Three quarters of everyone alive, holding a general-purpose networked computer. Since the early 2010s, improved hardware and faster wireless communication have kept driving that growth.

The record also notes the other side plainly. Smartphone use has been associated to some extent with convenience and life satisfaction — but a variety of negative effects on physical, mental and cognitive health have also been identified. Both findings sit in the same paragraph, which is roughly where the honest summary of this technology belongs.

10 key facts

  1. Smartphones largely replaced PDAs, media players, compact cameras, camcorders and GPS units as separate devices.
  2. Typical smartphone sensors include accelerometers, barometers, gyroscopes and magnetometers.
  3. By the mid-2020s manufacturers began adding satellite messaging and emergency services for areas with no cellular coverage.
  4. The slate shape with a large touchscreen and no physical keyboard spread after the iPhone's rise in the late 2000s.
  5. Over a billion smartphones were being sold globally every year as of 2014.
  6. 1.54 billion smartphone units shipped worldwide in 2019 alone.
  7. As of 2020, 75.05% of the world population were smartphone users.
  8. Smartphones support LTE, 5G NR, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and satellite navigation.
  9. Most modern smartphones support cloud storage, cloud synchronisation and virtual assistants.
  10. Smartphone use is linked both to convenience and life satisfaction and to negative physical, mental and cognitive health effects.

Key terms, decoded

accelerometer
A sensor that detects movement and acceleration, letting a device know when it is being moved or tilted.
app store
A single official marketplace from which a device downloads and installs software.
barometer
A sensor that measures air pressure, useful for altitude and weather.
capacitive touch screen
A screen that senses the electrical properties of a fingertip rather than requiring pressure.
cloud synchronisation
Keeping data automatically matched between a device and remote servers.
feature phone
An older, simpler mobile phone built mainly for calls and texts, without a full app ecosystem.
form factor
The physical size and shape of a device.
gyroscope
A sensor that measures rotation and orientation.
integrated circuit
A complete electronic circuit built onto a single small chip of silicon.
magnetometer
A sensor that detects magnetic fields, used as a digital compass.
mobile operating system
The core software that runs a phone and everything installed on it.
multi-touch
The ability of a screen to register several fingers at once, enabling gestures like pinch to zoom.
smartphone
A mobile phone that is also a full computer, running apps and connecting to the internet.
virtual assistant
Software that responds to spoken or typed requests and carries out tasks.
