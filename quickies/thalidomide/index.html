Thalidomide

It was sold over the counter for morning sickness, and one single dose was enough. The disaster it caused rewrote drug regulation worldwide — and decades later the same drug came back as a cancer treatment.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 15 terms decoded

Sold Without a Prescription for Morning Sickness

Chemie Grünenthal synthesised thalidomide in 1952 and initially discarded it. The company took it up again and launched it in West Germany in 1957 under the brand name Contergan — as an over-the-counter drug, available without a prescription.

It was promoted for anxiety, trouble sleeping, "tension" and morning sickness, and marketed as a safer alternative to barbiturates, the sedatives then in common use. It was believed to be safe in pregnancy.

The scale of the error is difficult to overstate, because of one detail: a single dose of thalidomide, regardless of dosage, is enough to cause harm to a developing foetus. There was no safe amount and no safe margin. A drug sold beside aspirin, taken by pregnant women precisely because it eased the nausea of early pregnancy, was doing its damage in exactly the window when it was most likely to be taken.

A Window of Sixteen Days

Thalidomide is a teratogen — a substance that causes birth defects — and an extraordinarily precise one. The critical exposure window falls roughly between days 20 and 36 after fertilisation, and what went wrong depended on exactly which day.

Exposure around day 22 affected the ears and face. Day 24 affected the arms. Exposure up to day 28 could damage the legs. The resulting conditions included phocomelia, a malformation of the limbs, and amelia, the absence of legs or arms altogether.

Between the late 1950s and early 1960s, over 10,000 children in 46 countries were born with severe deformities. About 40% of them died around the time of birth. Those who survived lived with limb, eye, urinary tract and heart problems. Some estimates put the total number of infants severely harmed as high as 20,000.

The Reviewer Who Said No Six Times

The United States was largely spared, and the reason was one civil servant. Frances Oldham Kelsey, a reviewer at the FDA, refused to approve thalidomide despite six separate requests from the company Richardson-Merrell. She was not satisfied by the safety data.

She was proved right in the most brutal way possible. Widukind Lenz presented evidence linking the deformities to thalidomide at a paediatric conference in Düsseldorf on 18 November 1961, and the drug was withdrawn from European markets that same month, with exact timing varying by country.

Kelsey received the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service from President Kennedy in 1962, and was inducted into the National Women's Hall of Fame in 2000. Her stubbornness is the reason the American figures are a footnote rather than a chapter — a rare case where a bureaucratic delay is the heroic act in the story.

The Disaster That Rewrote the Rulebook

The catastrophe forced governments to confront a gap they had left open: drugs could reach the public without meaningful proof of safety or effectiveness.

The legislative response came quickly and internationally:

- The Kefauver-Harris Amendment in the United States, 1962
- Directive 65/65/EEC in the European Community, 1965
- The Medicines Act in the United Kingdom, 1968

Together these mandated proof of efficacy — that a drug actually works — and comprehensive disclosure of side effects. Modern drug approval, as most countries now practise it, is in large part a monument built on this one failure.

There is a molecular postscript that makes the tragedy stranger. Thalidomide exists as a racemic mixture of two mirror-image forms called enantiomers: the (R)-form is the sedative, the (S)-form causes the birth defects. Manufacturing only the safe one would not have helped, because the body performs chiral inversion, converting each form into the other.

The Drug Came Back

In 1964, the Israeli physician Jacob Sheskin gave thalidomide to a leprosy patient despite the ban. The patient's erythema nodosum leprosum — a painful inflammatory complication of leprosy — improved dramatically. That observation launched clinical trials.

Thalidomide was approved in the United States in 1998 for erythema nodosum leprosum, and in 2006 for newly diagnosed multiple myeloma in combination with dexamethasone. It is used today for several cancers, graft-versus-host disease, and skin disorders, and appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Common side effects include sleepiness, rash and dizziness; severe ones include tumor lysis syndrome, blood clots and peripheral neuropathy.

Control is now extremely tight: pregnancy tests, dual contraception, and restricted prescriber networks. Even so, Brazil recorded at least 100 cases of thalidomide embryopathy between 2005 and 2010 despite strict controls — proof that the risk has been contained rather than eliminated.

10 key facts

  1. Thalidomide was launched in West Germany in 1957 as Contergan and sold over the counter without a prescription.
  2. A single dose of thalidomide, at any dosage, is enough to cause birth defects if taken during pregnancy.
  3. Over 10,000 children in 46 countries were born with severe deformities, and about 40% died around the time of birth.
  4. The critical damage window was roughly days 20 to 36 after fertilisation, with different days affecting ears, arms or legs.
  5. FDA reviewer Frances Oldham Kelsey refused approval six times, which is why the United States was largely spared.
  6. Kelsey received the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service from President Kennedy in 1962.
  7. Widukind Lenz presented the evidence linking thalidomide to the deformities on 18 November 1961, and withdrawal followed that month.
  8. The disaster produced the 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendment in the US, the 1965 EEC directive and the UK Medicines Act of 1968.
  9. Making only the non-harmful mirror-image form would not have helped, because the body converts each form into the other.
  10. Thalidomide was approved again in the US in 1998 and now appears on the WHO List of Essential Medicines.

Key terms, decoded

amelia
The complete absence of one or more limbs from birth.
barbiturate
An older class of sedative drug used to calm people or help them sleep, with a high risk of overdose.
chiral inversion
A process in the body that flips a molecule into its mirror-image form, so giving only one form does not keep it that way.
efficacy
Whether a treatment actually produces the benefit it claims, proven by testing rather than assumed.
enantiomer
One of two forms of a molecule that are mirror images of each other, like a left and right hand.
erythema nodosum leprosum
A painful inflammatory complication of leprosy that produces skin nodules and fever.
graft-versus-host disease
A condition where transplanted donor cells attack the tissues of the person who received them.
multiple myeloma
A cancer of a type of white blood cell found in bone marrow.
over-the-counter
Sold directly to the public without needing a doctor's prescription.
peripheral neuropathy
Damage to nerves outside the brain and spinal cord, causing numbness, tingling or pain, usually in the hands and feet.
phocomelia
A malformation in which the limbs are severely shortened, so hands or feet attach close to the body.
racemic mixture
A substance containing equal amounts of two mirror-image forms of the same molecule.
teratogen
A substance that disrupts the development of an unborn baby and causes birth defects.
thalidomide
A drug once sold as a sedative and anti-nausea treatment, now used for certain cancers and skin conditions under strict controls.
tumor lysis syndrome
A dangerous condition where many cancer cells break apart at once, flooding the blood with their contents.
