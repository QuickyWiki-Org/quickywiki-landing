Chernobyl Disaster

The reactor exploded during a safety test. The Soviet government said nothing for two days — until a monitor in Sweden picked up the radiation.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 11 terms decoded

A Safety Test That Destroyed the Reactor

On 26 April 1986, reactor 4 of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant, near Pripyat in Ukraine, exploded.

The bitter detail is what they were doing at the time. The disaster occurred during a test — a simulation of how the reactor would be cooled during a serious accident under blackout conditions. A safety exercise caused the accident it was rehearsing for.

The operators ran the test following an accidental drop in reactor power. Shutting down the reactor in those conditions exposed pervasive design flaws, which led to a power surge. Reactor components ruptured and lost coolant, and the resulting steam explosions and meltdown destroyed the reactor building. A reactor core fire followed, spreading radioactive contaminants across the Soviet Union and Europe. It is one of only two nuclear accidents rated at maximum severity on the International Nuclear Event Scale, alongside Fukushima in 2011.

Sweden Found Out First

The Soviet government did not publicly acknowledge the disaster for two days. What ended the silence was not an announcement but a detection: elevated radiation levels registered in Sweden.

A radioactive plume crossing international borders made concealment impossible. The disclosure was forced from outside.

The evacuation had begun before the admission. A 10-kilometre exclusion zone was established 36 hours after the accident, initially evacuating around 49,000 people. It was later expanded to 30 kilometres, resulting in the evacuation of roughly 68,000 more. Pripyat, a city built to serve the plant, was abandoned entirely and replaced by the purpose-built city of Slavutych.

Counting the Cost in People

The explosion itself killed two engineers and severely burned two others. An emergency operation began immediately to fight the fires and stabilise the reactor.

Of the 237 workers hospitalised, 134 showed symptoms of acute radiation syndrome, and 28 of them died within three months. Over the following decade a further 14 workers died of various causes, mostly unrelated to radiation. This remains the only instance in commercial nuclear power history where radiation-related fatalities occurred.

The longer-term toll is measured differently and disputed. As of 2005, 6,000 cases of childhood thyroid cancer had occurred among the affected populations — 15 of them fatal — with a large fraction attributed to the accident. Long-term death estimates range from up to 4,000 in Ukraine, Belarus and Russia according to the United Nations, to 16,000 across Europe.

Clean-Up Scheduled for 2065

The response involved more than 500,000 personnel and cost an estimated 18 billion rubles — about $85 billion in 2026 terms. Chernobyl remains the most destructive nuclear disaster and the most expensive disaster in history, with total estimated costs of US$700 billion.

Containment came in two stages, forty years apart. The sarcophagus was completed in December 1986, reducing the spread of contamination and providing radiological protection for crews working the undamaged reactors. Then in 2016–2018, the New Safe Confinement was constructed around the ageing sarcophagus, to enable removal of the reactor debris.

That removal is not finished. Clean-up is scheduled for completion by 2065 — nearly eighty years after the explosion, and a useful measure of what a few seconds of runaway reaction actually costs.

Key terms, decoded

acute radiation syndrome
The illness caused by receiving a very large dose of radiation over a short time.
contaminant
A harmful substance spread into an environment where it does not belong.
coolant
The fluid circulated through a reactor to carry heat away and stop it overheating.
exclusion zone
An area sealed off to the public because it is unsafe to enter or live in.
International Nuclear Event Scale
The scale used to rate the seriousness of nuclear accidents, running from 1 to 7.
meltdown
When a reactor core overheats so badly that its fuel begins to melt.
radiological protection
Measures taken to shield people from harmful radiation exposure.
reactor
The part of a nuclear power plant where a controlled chain reaction produces heat to generate electricity.
sarcophagus
The concrete and steel structure built to seal the destroyed reactor and contain its radiation.
steam explosion
A violent blast caused when water flashes instantly into steam and expands with enormous force.
thyroid cancer
Cancer of the gland in the neck that regulates metabolism, notably raised by radioactive iodine exposure.
