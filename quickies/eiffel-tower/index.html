Eiffel Tower

Famous artists called it useless and monstrous, and it was only meant to stand for 20 years. Then radio came along and saved the Iron Lady.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 3 terms decoded

The Tower Paris Once Hated

Today it is the symbol of Paris. But when it was being built, many of France's most famous artists wanted it stopped. In 1887, a group including the writer Guy de Maupassant and the architect Charles Garnier sent an angry letter to the government.

They called it a "useless and monstrous Eiffel Tower" and a "gigantic black smokestack" that would crush the beauty of Paris. Garnier later called it a "truly tragic street lamp".

Gustave Eiffel fired back by comparing it to the pyramids of Egypt. Why, he asked, would something admired in Egypt be ugly in Paris? Some critics changed their minds once it was finished. Maupassant, the story goes, ate lunch in the tower's restaurant every day, because it was the one place in Paris where he could not see it.

18,038 Pieces and 2.5 Million Rivets

The tower was built for the 1889 World's Fair, which marked 100 years since the French Revolution. The idea came from two engineers at Eiffel's company, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, who sketched a great iron pylon in 1884.

Building it was a feat of planning. The drawing office made 1,700 general drawings and 3,629 detailed ones for 18,038 different parts. Rivet holes were placed to within 1 millimetre. No part was shaped on site: if a piece did not fit, it went back to the factory.

In all, the parts were joined with 2.5 million rivets. When finished, it was by far the tallest human-made structure in the world, a title it kept for 41 years.

Saved by the Radio

Here is the surprise: the Eiffel Tower was never meant to last. It was built as a temporary structure and was due to be taken down in 1909, when Eiffel's 20-year deal ran out.

What saved it was science. The tower turned out to be perfect for radio telegraphy, sending messages through the air without wires. From 1910, the Paris Observatory used it to send the time to ships up to 5,000 kilometres away.

The radio station soon proved its worth. In 1914, at the start of World War I, a transmitter in the tower jammed German radio messages, which helped the Allies win the First Battle of the Marne. The tower had earned its place for good.

An Iron Lady That Moves

Locals call it "La dame de fer", the Iron Lady, because it is made of wrought iron. The iron frame alone weighs 7,300 tonnes. Even so, the design is so light for its size that the air in a box around the tower weighs almost as much as the iron itself.

The tower is not perfectly still, either. On a sunny day, the side facing the sun warms and expands, so the top can lean away from the sun by up to 18 centimetres.

Keeping it standing takes steady care. Every 7 years, workers apply 60 tonnes of paint to stop it rusting, and it has been repainted at least 19 times. Today it stands 330 metres tall and welcomed almost 5.9 million visitors in 2022.

10 key facts

  1. The Eiffel Tower was built as a temporary structure and was due to be taken down in 1909.
  2. Its usefulness for radio is what saved it from being demolished.
  3. The tower was held together with 2.5 million rivets.
  4. It was the tallest human-made structure in the world for 41 years, until the Chrysler Building was finished in 1930.
  5. On a sunny day, the top of the tower can lean away from the sun by up to 18 centimetres.
  6. The tower gets 60 tonnes of fresh paint every 7 years to stop it rusting.
  7. Gustave Eiffel had a private apartment at the top, where he welcomed guests including Thomas Edison.
  8. In 1914, a radio transmitter in the tower jammed German communications during World War I.
  9. A con artist named Victor Lustig "sold" the tower for scrap metal twice in 1925.
  10. Climbing the stairs to the second level takes about 600 steps.

Key terms, decoded

radio telegraphy
Sending coded messages through the air using radio waves, without needing any wires.
rivets
Short metal pins used to join metal plates together. They are heated, pushed through holes, and hammered flat at the end.
wrought iron
A tough, bendable type of iron with very little carbon, often used for building before steel became common.
