Tulip Mania

In 1637 a single flower bulb could cost ten times what a skilled craftsman earned in a year. Then the price collapsed in a month — and most of what you have heard about the ruin that followed is probably wrong.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 10 terms decoded

Ten Years' Wages for a Flower

Tulip mania was a stretch of the Dutch Golden Age when contract prices for bulbs of the newly fashionable tulip reached extraordinary levels. The major acceleration began in 1634 and ended in a dramatic collapse in February 1637.

At the peak, certain tulip bulbs sold for more than ten times the annual income of a skilled artisan. The most expensive variety was the Semper Augustus, which at its height sold for ƒ10,000 in guilders — roughly €130,000 in 2024 money — for a single bulb.

It is generally considered the first recorded speculative bubble in history. The bulbs did not become more useful, more beautiful or more plentiful in those three years. Only the expectations attached to them changed, and that turned out to be enough to move prices by orders of magnitude and then unmove them just as fast.

Trading Flowers That Were Still Underground

The mechanism mattered as much as the mania. Forward markets appeared in the Dutch Republic during the 17th century — agreements to buy something at a set price on a future date — and one of the most notable centred on tulips.

This is the crucial structural detail. Traders were not passing actual bulbs across a table. They were trading contracts on bulbs still in the ground, which could change hands repeatedly before any flower existed. A market where nothing physical needs to move can accelerate far faster than one where it does.

The context deserves stating plainly too. The Dutch Republic was one of the world's leading economic and financial powers in the 17th century, with the highest per capita income in the world from around 1600 to 1720. This was not a naive backwater discovering money for the first time — it was the most financially sophisticated society on Earth.

The Book That Made It Famous

The 1637 collapse was not a famous event for two centuries. It gained its lasting fame in 1841, with the publication of "Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds" by the Scottish journalist Charles Mackay.

Mackay supplied the details everyone now repeats. He wrote that at one point 5 hectares — about 12 acres — of land were offered for a single Semper Augustus bulb. He claimed many investors were ruined by the fall and that Dutch commerce suffered a severe shock.

His account is contested. Many modern scholars believe the mania was not as destructive as he described. Research is genuinely difficult here, because economic data from the 1630s is limited and much of what survives comes from biased and speculative sources. The story most people know is, in large part, a Victorian journalist's dramatic retelling of an event he did not witness, working from sources that were already unreliable.

Maybe Everyone Was Being Rational

The tidy moral — greedy crowds lose their minds — has come under sustained attack from economists offering rational explanations for the same price movements.

One argument is that this pattern is simply what new flowers do. Other species, such as the hyacinth, also commanded very high prices when first introduced, then fell as growers learned to propagate them and supply increased. High initial prices for a scarce novelty are not necessarily madness.

Another explanation is legal rather than botanical. Prices may have been driven up by expectations of a parliamentary decree allowing contracts to be voided for a small fee — which would have transformed a binding purchase into something closer to a cheap option, dramatically lowering a buyer's risk and making high bids much more reasonable.

What is not disputed is the phrase's afterlife. Tulip mania is now used metaphorically for any large economic bubble in which asset prices drift away from intrinsic value — a 17th-century flower market permanently loaned to every boom since.

  1. At the peak in February 1637, some tulip bulbs sold for more than ten times a skilled artisan's annual income.
  2. The Semper Augustus, the most prized variety, sold for as much as 10,000 guilders, roughly €130,000 in 2024 money.
  3. Tulip mania is generally considered the first recorded speculative bubble in history.
  4. The Dutch Republic had the highest per capita income in the world from around 1600 to 1720, so this was not an unsophisticated economy.
  5. Traders dealt in forward contracts on bulbs still in the ground rather than in the bulbs themselves.
  6. The episode only became famous in 1841 through Charles Mackay's book "Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds".
  7. Mackay claimed 12 acres of land were once offered for a single Semper Augustus bulb.
  8. Many modern scholars believe the mania was far less destructive than Mackay described.

  10. Prices may have risen partly because buyers expected a decree letting them void contracts cheaply, which would have slashed their risk.

Key terms, decoded

artisan
A skilled manual worker who makes things by hand, such as a carpenter or weaver.
asset bubble
Another term for a rapid, unsustainable rise in the price of an item people buy as an investment.
contract price
The price written into an agreement to buy something later, rather than the price paid on the spot today.
forward market
A market for agreements to buy or sell something at an agreed price on a future date.
guilder
The historical currency of the Netherlands, used before the euro.
intrinsic value
What something is genuinely worth based on its actual usefulness or the income it produces, rather than what people will currently pay.
per capita income
The average income per person in a place, found by dividing total income by the number of people.
propagate
To grow more plants from an existing one, for example by dividing bulbs or taking cuttings.
speculative bubble
A period when the price of something rises far above what it is really worth, driven by expectations of further rises, before collapsing.
void a contract
To cancel an agreement so that it no longer legally binds the people who made it.
