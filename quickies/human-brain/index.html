Human Brain

It holds more than 86 billion nerve cells, floats in fluid so it does not crush under its own weight, and is walled off from your own bloodstream by a barrier that guards it.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 24 terms decoded

Eighty-Six Billion Cells in One Organ

The human brain contains more than 86 billion neurons, plus a more or less equal number of other cells. That second half is easy to miss: the glial cells that support the neurons are roughly as numerous as the neurons themselves.

Brain activity is possible because of how those cells connect. Neurons link into neural pathways, then circuits, then elaborate network systems, and the whole thing runs on neurotransmission: neurons release neurotransmitters in response to nerve impulses.

The brain is the central organ of the nervous system, and together with the spinal cord it makes up the central nervous system. It controls most of the body's activities, processing and integrating information from the senses and coordinating the instructions sent back out.

Four Lobes, Four Different Jobs

The brain has three main parts: the cerebrum, the brainstem and the cerebellum. The cerebrum is the largest, and it splits into two hemispheres.

Each hemisphere has an inner core of white matter and an outer surface, the cerebral cortex, made of grey matter. The cortex itself has an outer layer called the neocortex, built from six layers of neurons, and an inner allocortex with three or four.

Each hemisphere divides into four lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital. The frontal lobe handles executive functions, including self-control, planning, reasoning and abstract thought. The occipital lobe is dedicated to vision. The two hemispheres are broadly similar, but language tends to sit on the left and visual-spatial ability on the right, and they are joined by nerve tracts, the largest being the corpus callosum.

The Fluid It Floats In

Inside the cerebrum sits the ventricular system: four interconnected chambers where cerebrospinal fluid is produced and circulated.

That fluid does something remarkable. The brain is suspended in it, which means it effectively floats rather than resting on the base of the skull under its own weight.

Protection comes in layers. The skull encloses it, the cerebrospinal fluid cushions it, and the blood-brain barrier isolates it from the bloodstream, screening what is allowed to cross. Beneath the cortex sit structures including the thalamus, hypothalamus, pineal gland and pituitary gland, along with limbic structures such as the amygdalae and hippocampi. Because these lie off the midline, they come in pairs: two hippocampi, two amygdalae.

What Can Go Wrong

All that protection is not absolute. The brain remains susceptible to damage, disease and infection.

Damage can come from trauma, or from a loss of blood supply, which is a stroke. It is also vulnerable to degenerative disorders including Parkinson's disease, dementias such as Alzheimer's disease, and multiple sclerosis. Psychiatric conditions including schizophrenia and clinical depression are thought to be associated with brain dysfunctions.

Tumours can occur too, both benign and malignant, and most of them originate elsewhere in the body before reaching the brain rather than starting there.

That last detail is worth sitting with. The organ most carefully walled off from the rest of the body is still most often invaded from it, which is a reminder that the skull, the fluid and the barrier are defences rather than guarantees.

From Phrenology to Brain Scans

Studying the brain has its own vocabulary. Neuroanatomy is the study of its structure, and neuroscience the study of its function.

The methods have changed enormously. Traditionally, much was learned from examining specimens from other animals under a microscope. The medical histories of people with brain injuries provided some of the sharpest insights into what individual regions actually do.

Modern work leans on imaging: functional neuroimaging and electroencephalography, or EEG, recordings. Not every attempt aged well. In the 19th century the pseudoscience of phrenology tried to map personality traits onto regions of the cortex. Meanwhile philosophy of mind has spent centuries on a question none of the scanners have answered: the nature of consciousness and the mind-body problem.

10 key facts

  1. The human brain contains more than 86 billion neurons, and roughly the same number of other cells.
  2. The brain floats in cerebrospinal fluid, which is produced and circulated in four interconnected ventricles.
  3. The blood-brain barrier isolates the brain from the bloodstream.
  4. The neocortex is built from six layers of neurons, while the inner allocortex has only three or four.
  5. The frontal lobe handles self-control, planning, reasoning and abstract thought, while the occipital lobe is dedicated to vision.
  6. Language ability tends to sit in the left hemisphere and visual-spatial ability in the right.
  7. Brain structures that lie off the midline come in pairs, so there are two hippocampi and two amygdalae.
  8. A stroke is damage caused by a loss of blood supply to the brain.
  9. Most brain tumours originate somewhere else in the body before reaching the brain.
  10. The 19th-century pseudoscience of phrenology tried to map personality traits onto regions of the cortex.

Key terms, decoded

allocortex
The inner part of the cerebral cortex, built from only three or four layers of neurons.
blood-brain barrier
A tight filter in the brain's blood vessels that controls which substances can pass from the bloodstream into brain tissue.
brainstem
The stalk connecting the brain to the spinal cord, made up of the midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata.
central nervous system
The brain and spinal cord together, the body's main control and processing centre.
cerebellum
A structure at the back of the brain involved in coordinating movement and balance.
cerebral cortex
The folded outer layer of the cerebrum, where much of the brain's higher processing happens.
cerebrospinal fluid
The clear liquid that surrounds and cushions the brain and spinal cord.
cerebrum
The largest part of the brain, divided into two halves and responsible for thought, sensation and voluntary movement.
corpus callosum
The thick bundle of nerve fibres connecting the brain's left and right hemispheres.
electroencephalography
A method of recording the brain's electrical activity through sensors placed on the scalp.
executive function
The set of mental skills used to plan, focus attention, hold back impulses and juggle tasks.
functional neuroimaging
Scanning techniques that show which parts of a living brain are active during a task.
glial cell
A supporting cell in the nervous system that protects and maintains neurons rather than carrying signals itself.
grey matter
Brain tissue made largely of nerve cell bodies, forming the outer surface of the cerebrum.
neocortex
The outer part of the cerebral cortex, built from six layers of neurons.
neuroanatomy
The study of the physical structure of the nervous system.
neuron
A nerve cell that carries signals through the body by means of electrical impulses and chemical messengers.
neuroscience
The scientific study of how the nervous system works.
neurotransmission
The process by which nerve cells pass signals to each other using chemical messengers.
neurotransmitter
A chemical messenger released by one nerve cell to pass a signal to the next.
phrenology
A discredited 19th-century idea that a person's character could be read from the shape of their skull.
stroke
Damage to the brain caused when its blood supply is cut off or interrupted.
ventricular system
The set of connected cavities inside the brain where cerebrospinal fluid is made and circulated.
white matter
Brain tissue made largely of the long fibres that carry signals between different regions.
