Populism

It elected presidents on the left and the right, became word of the year, and academics still can't agree what it is — the political style of 'the people versus the elite'.

3 min read · 8 key facts · 8 terms decoded

The People Versus the Elite

Populism is politics' most argued-about word — a contested concept covering political stances built on one master idea: the "common people", arrayed against a corrupt or self-serving elite. It travels with anti-establishment anger and suspicion of professional politics itself.

The most influential definition, the *ideational approach* championed by scholar Cas Mudde, calls populism a "thin-centred ideology": it divides society morally into the virtuous people and the corrupt elite — and stops there. Because the frame is thin, it attaches to anything. It has no fixed position on taxes, trade, or religion; it is a shape politics takes, not a program. That is precisely why populism keeps surprising people by appearing everywhere at once.

Farmers, Peasants, and the Birth of a Word

The word entered English in 1858 as a simple antonym for "aristocratic". Its political career began with two 19th-century movements an ocean apart. In Russia, the *narodniki* — literally "populists" — idealized the peasant commune and pushed agrarian reform, seeding later revolutionary currents. In the United States, the 1890s People's Party rallied indebted farmers against banks and railroads, demanding monetary expansion and progressive reforms.

Both movements claimed to speak for ordinary people abandoned by distant elites — the template ever since. And the template is ideologically ambidextrous: the label has been applied to figures across the spectrum, from Hugo Chávez and Bernie Sanders on the left to Donald Trump and Marine Le Pen on the right. The costume changes; the script — the people versus the elite — stays the same.

The 2016 Explosion

For decades populism was a specialist's term. Then came 2016 — the year of Brexit and Trump's election — and the word detonated. Academic output tells the story: roughly 160 publications on populism appeared across the entire 1950s; 2018 alone produced 615. The Cambridge Dictionary crowned "populism" its Word of the Year in 2017.

Scholars trace the wave to converging pressures: economic precarity and stagnant wages, cultural backlash against rapid social change, institutional failures that left voters feeling unrepresented, and a transformed media landscape letting leaders speak directly to audiences — no party machine, no editors, no filter. Beyond charismatic leaders, the style also powers parties like Greece's SYRIZA and Spain's Podemos, and leaderless eruptions like Occupy Wall Street and France's Yellow Vests. No single cause explains it everywhere — which is fitting, for a concept that resists every tidy definition.

Democracy's Medicine — or Its Poison?

Is populism good or bad for democracy? Political science's honest answer: both, depending on the dose and the patient. As a corrective, populism mobilizes excluded groups, forces complacent elites to hear real grievances, and shakes institutions that stopped listening. Movements dismissed as populist have repeatedly widened who politics is *for*.

As a threat, the record darkens. Once in power, populists may concentrate executive authority, undermine judicial independence, and attack press freedom — the majority's will wielded against the rules that protect everyone else, especially where institutions are fragile. And critics warn the label itself can mislead: stretched too far, it lumps genuine democratic outsiders together with authoritarians merely claiming to speak for "the people". The word remains contested because the stakes are: who counts as the people — and who gets to say so.

Key terms, decoded

anti-establishment
Opposed to the existing institutions and insiders of politics.
charismatic leader
A politician whose personal magnetism, rather than institutions or programs, binds followers to them.
elite
The small group perceived to hold concentrated power — political, economic, or cultural — over society.
judicial independence
Courts' freedom to rule by law without political interference.
narodniki
Nineteenth-century Russian populists who idealized the peasantry and pushed agrarian reform.
populism
A political style dividing society into the virtuous common people and a corrupt elite, and claiming to speak for the former.
thin ideology
A worldview that answers only a few questions and must attach itself to fuller ideologies for a complete program.
tyranny of the majority
The danger of a voting majority using its power to strip rights from minorities.
