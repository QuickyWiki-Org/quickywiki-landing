Steam engine

Steam-powered gadgets were known in the first century AD, yet it took until 1712 for an engine to do steady, useful work. Here is how steam went from curiosity to the power behind the modern world.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 7 terms decoded

Steam Power Is Older Than You Think

Steam power is far older than factories and trains. In the first century AD, Hero of Alexandria described the aeolipile, a simple steam-driven device. For centuries after that, the few steam engines people knew about were mostly experiments. Inventors used them to show what steam could do, not to get work done.

Slowly, more ideas appeared. A basic steam turbine was described by Taqi al-Din in Ottoman Egypt in 1551 and by Giovanni Branca in Italy in 1629. In 1606, the Spanish inventor Jerónimo de Ayanz received patents for 50 steam-powered inventions, including a water pump for draining flooded mines.

In 1690, the Frenchman Denis Papin first used a piston to raise weights. Then in 1698, Thomas Savery built a steam water pump, the first commercial steam-powered device. It was cheap, and it worked in mines and pumping stations. But it could not lift water very high, and its boiler was prone to exploding.

Newcomen's Engine Emptied the Mines

The big breakthrough came around 1712. Thomas Newcomen built the first commercially successful engine that could send continuous power to a machine. It was called the atmospheric engine, or "fire engine", and it used a piston, as Papin had proposed.

Its trick was clever. Steam filled a cylinder under a piston and was then condensed back into water. That left a partial vacuum, and the pressure of the outside air pushed the piston down. Because air pressure was the only useful force acting on it, the engine needed a very large cylinder.

Newcomen's engine was not very efficient, and it was mostly used for pumping water. Still, it let miners drain workings at depths that had been impractical before. Factories used it to pump water back up into a reservoir above a waterwheel, so the same water could turn the wheel again and again.

Watt's Separate Condenser Changed Everything

In 1764, James Watt made a critical improvement. He moved the spent steam into a separate condenser, a different vessel where it could cool down. This greatly increased the work he got from each unit of fuel. Boulton and Watt's early engines used half as much coal as John Smeaton's improved Newcomen engine.

To show off the gain, Watt introduced a measure called duty. It counted the work an engine delivered by burning one bushel of coal. The best Newcomen engines scored about 7 million, but most were closer to 5 million. Watt's early designs averaged about 17 million, a three-fold jump.

Watt then made his engine turn wheels, not just pump water. This rotary motion let factories be built away from rivers and sped up the Industrial Revolution. In 1788 he also adopted the centrifugal governor to help control engine speed, after his partner Boulton saw one on the equipment of a flour mill.

High Pressure Put Steam on Rails and Ships

Watt's patent blocked others from making high-pressure engines. Shortly after it expired in 1800, Richard Trevithick and, separately, Oliver Evans in 1801 introduced engines that used high-pressure steam. They were much more powerful for their size, and they could be made small enough for transport.

On 21 February 1804, the world's first railway journey took place. Trevithick's locomotive hauled 10 tonnes of iron, 70 passengers and five wagons from the Pen-y-darren ironworks to Abercynon in south Wales. In 1829, George Stephenson's Rocket won the Rainhill Trials. In 1830, the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened, using only steam power.

On water, steam led paddle steamers to replace sailing ships. Near the end of the 19th century, compound engines came into wide use. They passed steam through a series of ever larger cylinders to get more work from it. This mattered most on ships, where better efficiency meant carrying less heavy coal.

Steam Still Makes Most of Our Electricity

Piston steam engines were the main source of power until the early 20th century. Then better electric motors and internal combustion engines slowly took over. In power plants, steam turbines replaced piston engines because they cost less, ran faster and wasted less energy.

Steam never really left, though. Today most electric power is still made by steam turbines. In the United States, 90% of electric power is produced this way, using many different heat sources. And as of 2026, large piston steam engines are still being manufactured in Germany.

Safety has always been a worry. Boilers store a lot of energy, and boiler explosions have cost many lives in the past. Early safety valves could be adjusted by engine drivers, and some fastened them down to get more power, which led to many accidents. Modern valves are sealed so operators cannot tamper with them. In 2011, two German scientists at the University of Stuttgart revealed the world's smallest working "steam engine".

Key terms, decoded

aeolipile
An early steam device described in the first century AD. It showed that steam could create motion.
atmospheric engine
An early steam engine in which condensing steam made a vacuum and outside air pressure pushed the piston.
centrifugal governor
A device with spinning weights that helps control an engine's speed as the load changes.
compound engines
Steam engines that use the same steam in two or more cylinders in turn, getting more work from it.
duty
An old measure of steam engine efficiency. It counted how much work an engine did from burning one bushel of coal.
separate condenser
A vessel apart from the main cylinder where used steam is cooled back into water. It lets the cylinder stay hot and saves fuel.
steam turbines
Machines where steam spins bladed wheels to turn a shaft directly, instead of pushing a piston back and forth.
