Gravity

It is the weakest of the fundamental forces, and it built every star and galaxy in the universe. Physics still has no theory that fits it together with everything else.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 13 terms decoded

The Force That Assembled Everything

Gravity — from the Latin *gravitas*, meaning weight — is a fundamental interaction, describable as the force drawing material objects toward each other.

What that plain definition hides is what gravity actually did. Gravitational attraction between clouds of primordial hydrogen and clumps of dark matter in the early universe caused the gas to coalesce, eventually condensing and fusing to form stars. At larger scales the same process produced galaxies and clusters.

So gravity is the primary driver of the large-scale structure of the universe. Every star that exists was assembled by things falling toward each other. Gravity also has infinite range — its effects weaken with distance but never stop entirely, which is why the force between two objects is technically never zero, however far apart they are.

Two Descriptions, Both Correct

Gravity is described by the general theory of relativity, proposed by Albert Einstein in 1915, which explains gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by the uneven distribution of mass. Objects are not being pulled so much as following the straightest available path through bent space.

The most extreme example of that curvature is a black hole, from which nothing — not even light — escapes once past the event horizon.

But relativity is not what most calculations use. For most applications, gravity is sufficiently well approximated by Newton's law of universal gravitation: an attractive force between any two bodies, proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Newton's version is not wrong so much as an excellent approximation, and it is far easier to compute — so engineers keep using a description that was superseded over a century ago.

Weight, Tides and Weather

On the surface of a planetary body such as Earth, gravity operates toward the centre of the body — and is modified by the centrifugal effects of the body's rotation. That is why weight varies slightly depending on where you stand on the planet.

In this everyday context, gravity is what gives physical objects weight, and it drives systems that are rarely credited to it:

- Surface water waves
- Lunar tides
- A substantial contribution to weather patterns

Gravitational weight also has important biological functions. It guides the growth of plants through gravitropism — how a root knows to go down and a shoot to go up — and influences the circulation of fluids in multicellular organisms. A force usually discussed in terms of planets is also the reason a seedling can orient itself in the dark.

The Piece That Will Not Fit

Gravity remains the outstanding unsolved problem in fundamental physics, and the reason is a mismatch of frameworks rather than a lack of data.

Scientists are looking for a theory that describes gravity within quantum mechanics — quantum gravity — which would unify gravity with the other known fundamental interactions inside a single mathematical framework, a theory of everything.

The difficulty is that the two best-tested theories in science do not agree about what gravity is. General relativity treats it as smooth geometry; quantum mechanics describes everything else in discrete packets and probabilities. Both work superbly in their own domain, and both are needed exactly where the other breaks down — inside black holes, and at the beginning of the universe. That gap has stayed open for a century.

10 key facts

  1. Gravitational attraction between primordial hydrogen and dark matter caused the first stars to form.
  2. Gravity is the primary driver of the large-scale structure of the universe, including galaxies and clusters.
  3. Gravity has infinite range, weakening with distance but never reaching zero.
  4. Einstein's 1915 general theory of relativity describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass.
  5. Newton's law of universal gravitation remains an excellent approximation for most practical purposes.
  6. Newton's law makes the force proportional to the masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance.
  7. Surface gravity is modified by the centrifugal effect of a planet's rotation, so weight varies by location.
  8. Gravity drives water waves, lunar tides and contributes substantially to weather patterns.
  9. Gravitropism, the process by which gravity guides plant growth, is a biological function of gravitational weight.
  10. There is still no accepted theory of quantum gravity uniting gravity with the other fundamental interactions.

Key terms, decoded

centrifugal effect
The outward tendency felt by anything on a spinning body, which slightly counteracts surface gravity.
dark matter
Matter that does not emit light but reveals itself through its gravitational pull.
event horizon
The boundary around a black hole beyond which nothing can escape.
fundamental interaction
One of the basic forces of nature that cannot be explained in terms of anything more basic.
general relativity
Einstein's theory describing gravity as the bending of space and time by mass and energy.
gravitropism
The way plants sense gravity and grow their roots downward and shoots upward.
gravity
The attraction that draws objects with mass toward each other.
inverse square law
A relationship where a quantity weakens with the square of distance, so doubling the distance quarters the effect.
primordial hydrogen
The simplest gas, formed shortly after the Big Bang, from which the first stars condensed.
quantum gravity
The sought-after theory that would describe gravity using the rules of quantum mechanics.
quantum mechanics
The physics describing matter and energy at the smallest scales, in discrete packets and probabilities.
spacetime
The combined four-dimensional fabric of space and time that mass can curve.
theory of everything
A hypothetical single framework that would describe all fundamental forces together.
