Fever

A fever is not the illness attacking you. It is your own brain deliberately turning up the thermostat, and the evidence on whether you should bring it down is genuinely surprising.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 12 terms decoded

Your Brain Moves the Thermostat

A fever is a symptom of an anti-infection defence mechanism. Your body temperature rises because the hypothalamus, the part of the brain that regulates temperature, has raised its own target set point. The heat is not the infection overwhelming you. It is a deliberate instruction.

That explains the strangest part of having one. When the set point jumps, your actual temperature is suddenly too low by the new standard, so the body behaves exactly as it would in the cold: muscle contractions increase, you shiver, and you feel freezing while running hot. When the set point drops back to normal, the reverse happens and you feel hot, flush and start to sweat.

There is no single agreed upper limit for a normal temperature, with sources using values between 37.2 and 38.3 °C. Fevers also have a natural ceiling and do not typically go higher than 41 to 42 °C.

Fever Is Not Overheating

Fever and hyperthermia look similar and are completely different events, and confusing them is dangerous.

In a fever, the set point moves and the body works to reach it. In hyperthermia, the set point stays exactly where it was and the body overheats anyway, because it is producing too much heat or shedding too little. Heat stroke and adverse drug reactions are typical causes.

The clinical stakes are high. Hyperthermia can quickly lead to death and does not respond to antipyretic medications, the drugs that bring a fever down, because there is no raised set point for them to lower. Fever medication simply does not address the problem. The distinction can be genuinely difficult to make in an emergency setting and is often established by working out the possible causes rather than by the temperature reading itself. Hyperthermia requires treatment. A fever usually does not.

How the Instruction Gets Sent

The trigger is a pyrogen, a substance that induces fever, and it works by causing the release of prostaglandin E2, which acts on the hypothalamus and resets the target temperature.

Pyrogens come from both outside and inside. External ones are microbial, most commonly endotoxins produced by Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli. Internal ones are cytokines released by your own immune cells, chiefly interleukin 1 and interleukin 6, along with tumour necrosis factor. These travel in the bloodstream to regions of the brain where they can cross more easily than in areas protected by the blood-brain barrier.

Once the new set point is in place, the body pulls every lever it has. Blood vessels near the skin constrict, which both conserves heat and makes you feel cold. Norepinephrine increases heat production in brown fat. Shivering raises the metabolic rate. Heart rate and blood pressure both rise. The result is a body methodically climbing toward a temperature it has decided it wants.

Should You Bring It Down?

Treatment to reduce a fever is generally not required. That is the plain guidance, and it sits awkwardly with how most people behave.

Treating the associated pain and inflammation may genuinely be useful and help someone rest, and medicines such as ibuprofen or paracetamol do that as well as lowering temperature. But on the fever itself, while it evolved as a defence mechanism, treating it does not appear to improve or worsen outcomes either way.

The caveats matter. Children younger than three months need medical attention, as might anyone with a compromised immune system or other concerning symptoms. Fever is one of the most common medical signs there is, involved in about 30% of children's healthcare visits and occurring in up to 75% of adults who are seriously ill. It also brings its own baggage: sickness behaviour such as lethargy, loss of appetite and inability to concentrate, the intense and confusing nightmares known as fever dreams, and rarely a febrile seizure, which is more common in young children. Doctors have a name for the mismatch between concern and risk. They call it "fever phobia".

10 key facts

  1. A fever happens because the brain raises its own target temperature, not because the body has lost control of it.
  2. You feel cold during a rising fever because your actual temperature is below the new set point your brain has chosen.
  3. There is no single agreed upper limit for normal body temperature, with sources using values between 37.2 and 38.3 °C.
  4. Fevers do not typically rise above 41 to 42 °C.
  5. Hyperthermia is not a fever, can quickly become fatal, and does not respond to fever-reducing drugs at all.
  6. Treating a fever does not appear to improve or worsen outcomes.
  7. Fever is involved in about 30 percent of children's healthcare visits and occurs in up to 75 percent of seriously ill adults.
  8. Children younger than three months with a fever require medical attention.
  9. The most common external fever triggers are endotoxins produced by bacteria such as E. coli.
  10. Doctors use the term fever phobia for the tendency of parents and professionals to worry about fever more than it usually warrants.

Key terms, decoded

antipyretic
A medicine that lowers a fever.
blood-brain barrier
The tight lining of blood vessels in the brain that blocks most substances in the blood from reaching brain tissue.
brown fat
A specialised body fat that generates heat directly rather than storing energy. Also called brown fat.
cytokine
A signalling molecule released by immune cells to coordinate the body's response to infection.
endotoxin
A toxic component of the outer wall of certain bacteria, released when they break down.
febrile seizure
A convulsion brought on by a rapid rise in temperature, most often in young children.
fever
A raised body temperature caused by the brain deliberately setting a higher target, usually as a defence against infection.
hyperthermia
Dangerous overheating in which body temperature rises above the brain's target without that target having changed.
hypothalamus
The small region of the brain that regulates body temperature, hunger, thirst and sleep.
pyrogen
Any substance that triggers a fever.
set point
The target temperature the brain is currently trying to hold the body at.
sickness behaviour
The cluster of lethargy, low mood, appetite loss and poor concentration that accompanies being ill.
