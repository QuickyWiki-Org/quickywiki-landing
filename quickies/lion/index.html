Lion

It is the only big cat on Earth that lives in a family, and the only one where the females do most of the hunting. Its kingdom used to stretch from Southeast Europe to India.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 8 terms decoded

The Only Big Cat That Lives in a Team

Almost every wild cat on the planet hunts alone. The lion does not. It is a social species that forms groups called prides, and that single difference shapes everything about how it lives.

A pride is built around related females and their cubs, plus one adult male or a few, who are unrelated to the females. The females stay. The males are the outsiders who arrive, and adult males often have to compete just to keep or gain their place in the group.

Groups of female lions usually hunt together. That teamwork is what lets a pride bring down prey far larger than any single cat could handle, which is mostly medium-sized and large hooved mammals.

Built for the Open Grass

The lion, Panthera leo, is a large cat with a muscular, broad-chested body, a short rounded head, round ears, and a dark hairy tuft right at the tip of its tail. It lives in grasslands, savannahs and shrublands.

It is sexually dimorphic, meaning males and females look clearly different. Adult males are larger, and they carry the mane that runs from the head down over the shoulders and chest.

One habit sets it apart from its relatives: the lion is usually more active in daylight than other wild cats. But that is flexible. Where lions are persecuted by people, they shift their schedule and become active at night and at twilight instead. As an apex predator and a keystone predator, its presence shapes the whole ecosystem around it.

From Southeast Europe to a Single Indian Forest

During the Neolithic period the lion ranged across Africa and Eurasia, all the way from Southeast Europe to India. That is a staggering territory, and almost none of it is left.

Today the lion is reduced to fragmented populations in sub-Saharan Africa, plus one surviving population in western India. It has been listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List since 1996, because populations in African countries have fallen by roughly 43% since the early 1990s.

The cause of the decline is not fully understood, but two pressures stand out: habitat loss and conflict with humans. Outside designated protected areas, lion populations are simply untenable.

Why We Put Lions on Flags

No animal has been carved, painted and printed more often. The lion appears in sculptures and paintings, on national flags, and throughout literature and film. It is one of the most widely recognised animal symbols in all of human culture.

The meaning has been remarkably consistent across the world: power and royalty. Lions figure prominently as symbols and as deities in ancient religions, in cultures that had never met each other.

The fascination went beyond art. Lions have been kept in menageries since the time of the Roman Empire, and since the late 18th century they have been the key species that zoological gardens across the world wanted on display.

Key terms, decoded

apex predator
An animal at the very top of its food chain, with no natural predators hunting it.
IUCN Red List
The world's main inventory of the conservation status of animal and plant species.
keystone predator
A predator whose presence holds an entire ecosystem in balance, so that removing it changes the whole community of species.
menagerie
A private collection of captive wild animals kept for display, an early forerunner of the zoo.
pride
A social group of lions, made up of related females and their cubs together with one or a few adult males.
savannah
A grassy plain with scattered trees, warm all year, found in tropical and subtropical regions.
sexually dimorphic
When males and females of the same species look noticeably different from each other in size, colour or body features.
Vulnerable
A conservation status meaning a species faces a high risk of extinction in the wild if the pressures on it continue.
