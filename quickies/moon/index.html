Moon

You have never seen one side of it, it is made largely of shredded Earth, and the fact that it exactly covers the Sun during an eclipse is a coincidence with no deeper reason.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 11 terms decoded

The Side You Will Never See

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth. Its rotation period exactly matches its orbital period, a 1:1 spin and orbit resonance called synchronous rotation, so it keeps nearly the same face turned toward us forever. The half pointed at us is the near side, the other is the far side.

Calling the far side the "dark side" is simply wrong. It is illuminated exactly as often as the near side, once every 29.5 Earth days. It is hidden, not dark.

The lock is not perfect, which is a small mercy. Because the Moon's orbit is slightly elliptical and tilted, it appears to rock and nod very slightly from our point of view, an effect called libration. Over time and from different places on Earth, that wobble reveals about 59% of the Moon's surface rather than a flat 50%. On occasion you can glimpse up to about 101% of a full moon's worth, sneaking a look around the edge.

It Is Mostly Made of Us

Isotope dating of lunar samples suggests the Moon formed around 50 million years after the origin of the Solar System. The prevailing explanation is violent: a giant impact between the proto-Earth and a Mars-sized body named Theia, about 4.51 billion years ago.

The oblique collision blasted material into orbit, which then accreted just beyond Earth's Roche limit and became the Moon. The impact released enough energy to liquefy both the ejected material and Earth's crust, forming a magma ocean, and the newly formed Moon had a magma ocean of its own, estimated at somewhere between 500 and 1,737 km deep.

The strongest evidence is chemical. Mars and Vesta have very different oxygen and tungsten isotope compositions from Earth, but Earth and the Moon are nearly identical, which is hard to explain unless they share material. Every rival theory fails somewhere: fission would need an impossibly fast-spinning Earth, capture would need an absurdly extended atmosphere, and co-formation cannot explain why the Moon is so short of metals.

Why the Sea Moves

Earth and the Moon are bound by gravity that pulls harder on the sides facing each other. The resulting tidal forces are the main driver of Earth's tides, and they are the same forces that dragged the Moon into always showing us one face.

The relationship is genuinely mutual, which is not how it is usually pictured. Earth and the Moon share a centre of mass called the barycenter, and it sits about 5,000 km from Earth's centre, roughly three quarters of the way out from the middle to the surface. Both bodies orbit that point, so Earth wobbles around it once for every orbit the Moon makes.

That arrangement is slowly unwinding. Tidal friction constantly drains energy from the rotating pair, pushing the barycenter outward, and eventually it will lie outside the Earth entirely. The Moon's crust feels the same forces in reverse, flexing through tides of around 10 cm over 27 days.

The Coincidence That Gives Us Eclipses

The Moon's apparent size in our sky is about the same as the Sun's. Nothing requires that. It is the reason a total solar eclipse works at all, with the Moon covering the Sun almost exactly.

It is not a fixed match, because the orbit is elliptical. The distance ranges from about 356,400 km at perigee to 406,700 km at apogee, which makes the Moon's apparent size fluctuate by up to 14%.

Another mismatch between reputation and reality is brightness. The Moon is the brightest object in Earth's night sky, but only because it is large and close. The reflectance of its surface is comparable to that of asphalt. It looks bright the way a road looks bright under a streetlight. The favour is returned at night: Earthshine makes the lunar near side about 43 times brighter, and sometimes 55 times brighter, than an Earth night lit by a full moon.

The Only Place We Have Stood

The Moon is the only celestial body beyond Earth that humans have visited, and the first target of every kind of spaceflight milestone.

The sequence began in 1959 with the Soviet Luna 1 flyby and the deliberate impact of Luna 2, followed in 1966 by the first soft landing with Luna 9 and the first orbital insertion with Luna 10. Humans reached lunar orbit with Apollo 8 on 24 December 1968, and the surface with Apollo 11 on 20 July 1969.

By 1972, six Apollo missions had landed twelve people, staying up to three days at a time. Then it stopped for decades. Renewed robotic exploration, particularly work confirming the presence of water on the Moon, has driven plans to return, with the Artemis programme of the 2020s including a crewed flyby with Artemis II in 2026.

10 key facts

  1. The Moon's far side is not dark. It is lit exactly as often as the near side, once every 29.5 Earth days.
  2. Because the Moon rocks slightly as it orbits, about 59 percent of its surface is visible from Earth rather than half.
  3. The Moon is thought to have formed from debris blasted off Earth by a collision with a Mars-sized body called Theia.
  4. Earth and the Moon have nearly identical oxygen and tungsten isotope compositions, unlike Mars or Vesta.
  5. The shared centre of mass of the Earth and Moon sits about 5,000 km from Earth's centre, so Earth wobbles around it every orbit.
  6. Tidal friction is slowly pushing that shared centre of mass outward, and eventually it will lie outside the Earth.
  7. The Moon's surface reflects light about as well as asphalt does.
  8. The Moon's distance varies enough that its apparent size in the sky changes by up to 14 percent.
  9. Earthshine makes night on the Moon's near side about 43 times brighter than a full-moon night on Earth.
  10. Twelve people have walked on the Moon, all of them between 1969 and 1972.

Key terms, decoded

apogee
The point in an orbit where the Moon is furthest from Earth.
barycenter
The shared centre of mass that two orbiting bodies both actually revolve around.
far side
The hemisphere of the Moon permanently facing away from Earth.
isotope dating
Working out the age of a rock by measuring the proportions of different forms of an element inside it.
libration
The slight apparent rocking of the Moon that lets us see a little way around its edges over time.
near side
The hemisphere of the Moon permanently facing Earth.
perigee
The point in an orbit where the Moon is closest to Earth.
reflectance
How much light a surface reflects rather than absorbs.
Roche limit
The distance within which a large moon would be torn apart by its planet's gravity.
synchronous rotation
Rotating once in exactly the time it takes to complete one orbit.
tidally locked
When a moon's spin has been slowed by gravity until it turns exactly once per orbit, so it always shows the same face to its planet.
