Human Rights

They are not granted by any government, which is exactly why no government can take them away. The modern idea of them was written down in response to the worst thing humans had ever done.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 8 terms decoded

Rights Nobody Has to Give You

Human rights are moral principles or norms that set standards of human behaviour, and they are often protected by both national and international law.

The crucial word is inherent. These rights are considered inherent and inalienable, meaning they belong to every individual simply by virtue of being human. They are not a gift from a state, so a state cannot revoke them by decree.

They also cover far more ground than most people assume. The range is civil, political, economic, social and cultural, and it includes the right to life, freedom of speech, protection against enslavement, and the right to education. That last one matters: education sits alongside free speech, not below it.

The Document Written After the War

Ideas related to human rights predate modernity by a long way. But the modern concept gained real prominence only after the Second World War, and specifically in response to the atrocities of the Holocaust.

That response took a concrete form. In 1948 the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, usually shortened to the UDHR.

The declaration outlined a comprehensive framework of rights that countries are encouraged to protect, setting a global standard for human dignity, freedom and justice. Everything downstream flows from it: the UDHR has since inspired numerous international treaties and national laws aimed at promoting and protecting human rights worldwide.

The Argument That Never Ended

The principle of universal human rights is widely accepted. The details are not, and the disagreements are serious ones.

Debates persist over which rights should take precedence when they collide, how they should actually be implemented, and how they apply across different cultural contexts.

One of the sharpest criticisms comes from cultural relativism, which argues that individually framed human rights are a poor fit for societies that prioritise a communal or collectivist identity, and that they may conflict with certain cultural or traditional practices. The argument has not been settled, and it shapes how the framework is received around the world.

Who Actually Enforces Them

Despite the disputes, human rights remain a central focus of international relations and of legal frameworks around the world.

The work of monitoring and enforcing them is spread across three very different kinds of body: institutions such as the United Nations, a wide range of non-governmental organisations, and national bodies set up specifically for the purpose.

That spread is deliberate. No single actor holds the whole responsibility, which makes the system harder to shut down but also harder to make binding. It is why human rights are simultaneously one of the most agreed-upon ideas in the world and one of the most argued-about.

9 key facts

  1. Human rights are considered inherent and inalienable, belonging to a person simply because they are human.
  2. They cover civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights, not just political freedoms.
  3. The right to education is a human right, listed alongside the right to life and freedom of speech.
  4. The modern concept rose to prominence after World War II, particularly in response to the Holocaust.
  5. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1948.
  6. The UDHR has inspired numerous international treaties and national laws around the world.
  7. Ideas related to human rights existed long before the modern era.
  8. Cultural relativism argues that individually framed rights may not suit societies built around communal identity.
  9. Enforcement is shared between the United Nations, non-governmental organisations and national bodies.

Key terms, decoded

cultural relativism
The view that beliefs and practices should be judged within their own culture rather than by outside standards.
Holocaust
The systematic mass murder of millions of people, primarily Jews, carried out by Nazi Germany during the Second World War.
inalienable
Describing something that cannot be taken away from a person or given up by them.
inherent
Belonging to something naturally and permanently, rather than being added or granted from outside.
non-governmental organisation
An independent group that works on public issues without being run by any government.
treaty
A formal written agreement between countries that binds them under international law.
United Nations General Assembly
The main assembly of the United Nations, where every member country has a seat and a vote.
Universal Declaration of Human Rights
A document adopted by the United Nations in 1948 setting out a shared list of rights that all people are entitled to.
