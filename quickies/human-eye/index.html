Human eye

Each of your eyes has a blind spot, and your eyes never truly stop moving, even when you stare. Without those tiny wiggles, what you look at would fade away.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 11 terms decoded

A Camera Made of Living Tissue

Your eye works a lot like a camera. Light first passes through the cornea, the clear front window of the eye, which does most of the focusing. Then it goes through the pupil, a hole whose size is controlled by the coloured ring around it, the iris.

Next comes the lens, which fine-tunes the focus. Finally, the light lands on the retina at the back of the eye, where the image is picked up and sent to the brain through the optic nerve.

The pupil works just like a camera's opening. It is usually about 4 millimetres wide, but it can shrink to 2 millimetres in bright light and widen to 8 millimetres in the dark. The adult eye is only about 24 millimetres across.

Rods for the Dark, Cones for Colour

The retina is packed with cells that turn light into electrical signals. There are two main kinds, and they share the work.

Rods handle dim light. They let you see in the dark, but only in shades of grey and without much detail. Cones need brighter light, but they give you sharp detail and colour.

A third, more recently discovered type of cell helps too. It responds to all light levels and helps control how much light reaches the retina. It also helps set your body clock, known as your circadian rhythm, by affecting the sleep hormone melatonin. So your eyes do more than let you see. They also help tell your body when it is day and when it is night.

The Blind Spot in Every Eye

Here is something strange: each of your eyes has a spot where it cannot see anything at all. It is where the optic nerve leaves the eye, and there are no light-sensing cells there. This blind spot sits about 15 degrees to the side of where you are looking, toward your nose, and is roughly 7.5 degrees high and 5.5 degrees wide.

That is a real gap in your view of the world, sitting in every eye you have.

Your eyes also never stop moving. Even when you stare at one spot, they make tiny drifts and flickers. This keeps fresh light falling on the sensing cells. Without these movements, the cells would stop sending signals, and what you are looking at would fade from view.

From Starlight to Sunshine

The eye can handle an astonishing range of light. The brightest light it can use for normal seeing is about one hundred trillion times brighter than the dimmest light it can detect.

Adjusting to darkness takes time, though. When you walk into a dark room, the first changes happen in about four seconds. But the full shift, called dark adaptation, is about 80% complete after thirty minutes. A sudden flash of light forces the whole process to start again.

Together, your two eyes can see about 214 degrees from side to side. Babies' eyes grow fast too, from about 16 millimetres wide at birth to full adult size by age 12.

Why Most Eyes Are Brown

Brown is by far the most common eye colour in the world. About 79% of people have brown eyes. They get their colour from a lot of melanin, a dark natural pigment, in the iris. In many parts of the world, brown is almost the only eye colour you will see.

Blue eyes are much rarer, found in about 8 to 10% of people around the world. They are most common in northern and eastern Europe, especially around the Baltic Sea.

Eye colour was once thought to be passed down in a simple way. Scientists now know it is controlled by several genes working together, a type of trait called polygenic.

10 key facts

  1. Each human eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve leaves the eye.
  2. The cornea does most of the focusing in the human eye.
  3. Rods help us see in dim light, while cones give us colour and sharp detail.
  4. Your eyes keep making tiny movements even when you stare, or what you see would fade.
  5. The pupil can widen from about 2 millimetres in bright light to 8 millimetres in the dark.
  6. Full adjustment to darkness is about 80% complete after thirty minutes.
  7. The eye can handle light across a range of about one hundred trillion to one.
  8. Human eyes reach their full adult size by about age 12.
  9. About 79% of people in the world have brown eyes.
  10. About 8 to 10% of people in the world have blue eyes.

Key terms, decoded

blind spot
The small area in each eye's view where nothing can be seen, because no light-sensing cells are there.
circadian rhythm
The body's natural daily cycle that controls when we feel awake or sleepy.
Cones
Light-sensing cells in the retina that need brighter light and let us see colour and detail.
cornea
The clear, curved outer layer at the front of the eye that bends light as it enters.
dark adaptation
The slow process by which the eyes become more sensitive after moving into darkness.
melanin
A natural dark pigment that gives colour to eyes, skin and hair.
optic nerve
The bundle of nerve fibres that carries signals from the eye to the brain.
polygenic
Controlled by many genes acting together, rather than by a single gene.
pupil
The black opening in the centre of the eye that lets light in and changes size with brightness.
retina
The thin, light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye that turns light into signals for the brain.
Rods
Light-sensing cells in the retina that work in dim light but do not see colour.
