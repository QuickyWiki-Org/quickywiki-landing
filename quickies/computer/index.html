Computer

For over three centuries, a computer was a person, usually a woman, doing arithmetic by hand. The machine stole the job title, and then the world.

3 min read · 8 key facts · 10 terms decoded

The Job Title That a Machine Stole

The word computer is far older than any machine, and it originally meant a person. According to the Oxford English Dictionary, the first known use appears in a 1613 book by the English writer Richard Brathwait, praising "the truest computer of Times, and the best Arithmetician that euer breathed".

That meaning held for over three hundred years. A computer was someone who carried out calculations by hand, often for astronomy, navigation or engineering. Later in that period, women were frequently hired for the work because they could be paid less than men. By 1943, most human computers were women.

Only around the mid-20th century did the word shift to the machine. The Online Etymology Dictionary dates the sense of "calculating machine" to 1897, and the modern sense of a programmable digital electronic computer to 1945.

Ancient Gears That Could Do Maths

People have built calculating aids for thousands of years. The earliest was probably a tally stick. Around the Fertile Crescent, clay spheres and cones sealed in hollow containers stood in for counts of grain or livestock.

Then came the abacus. The Roman version descended from devices used in Babylonia as early as 2400 BCE. In a medieval European counting house, clerks laid a checkered cloth over a table and moved markers around it by fixed rules to total up sums of money.

The most startling object of all is the Antikythera mechanism, believed to be the earliest known mechanical analog computer, built to calculate astronomical positions. It was pulled from a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera in 1901 and dates to roughly 100 BCE. Nothing of comparable complexity appears again until the fourteenth century.

Five Switches, Each Smaller Than the Last

The modern story is really the story of one component getting smaller and faster. Early in the Industrial Revolution, mechanical devices were built to automate long, tedious jobs such as guiding patterns for looms. By the early 20th century, more sophisticated electrical machines were doing specialised analog calculations.

Digital electronic calculating machines arrived during World War II, and the switch at their heart kept changing: electromechanical relays in the 1930s, thermionic valves in the same decade, semiconductor transistors in the 1940s, the MOSFET in the 1950s, and finally monolithic integrated circuits from the 1950s onward.

Each step meant smaller, more efficient circuits, which led to the microprocessor and the microcomputer revolution of the 1970s. Moore's law noted that transistor counts doubled roughly every two years. In the 1930s computers numbered only a few. Today they run key fobs, microwave ovens and supercomputers alike.

What Is Actually Inside the Box

Strip away the casing and a modern computer is surprisingly consistent. There is at least one processing element, usually a central processing unit in the form of a microprocessor, plus some computer memory, typically semiconductor memory chips.

The processing element does the arithmetic and logic. A sequencing and control unit can change the order of operations in response to information it has stored, which is exactly what makes a computer programmable rather than merely fast.

Everything else is peripheral devices. Input devices such as keyboards, mice and joysticks bring information in. Output devices such as monitors and printers send results out. Touchscreens do both. Together they let information come from outside and let results be saved and retrieved later.

8 key facts

  1. The first known use of the word computer, in 1613, referred to a person who did calculations, not a machine.
  2. By 1943, most human computers were women, partly because they could be paid less than men for the same work.
  3. The Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek analog computer for astronomical positions, dates to around 100 BCE and was discovered in a shipwreck in 1901.
  4. No device as complex as the Antikythera mechanism reappeared until the fourteenth century.
  5. The Roman abacus developed from devices used in Babylonia as early as 2400 BCE.
  6. The planisphere, a star chart that worked as an analog computing aid, was invented by Abu Rayhan al-Biruni in the early 11th century.
  7. In the 1930s the world held only a few computers, while today they are built into key fobs, microwaves and remote controls.
  8. Moore's law observed that transistor counts doubled about every two years, driving the digital revolution.

Key terms, decoded

analog computer
A calculating machine that works with continuously varying physical quantities such as gears or voltages, rather than with separate digits.
central processing unit
The part of a computer that carries out the arithmetic, logic and control of a program. Often called the CPU.
integrated circuit
A whole electronic circuit built onto a single small piece of semiconductor material instead of assembled from separate parts.
microprocessor
A central processing unit built onto a single chip.
MOSFET
A specific type of transistor that can be made extremely small and packed together in huge numbers, which made modern chips possible.
peripheral device
Any piece of equipment attached to a computer to get information in or out, such as a keyboard, monitor or printer.
program
A stored set of instructions that tells a computer which operations to perform and in what order.
semiconductor
A material that conducts electricity only under certain conditions, which is what lets it act as a controllable switch.
thermionic valve
An early glass tube component used as an electronic switch or amplifier before transistors replaced it.
transistor
A tiny electronic switch that controls the flow of current, and the basic building block of modern computers.
