Parachuting

The first famous jump came from a balloon over Paris in 1797, and today people leap from the edge of space at over 1,300 km/h.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 6 terms decoded

A Silk Leap Over Paris

On 22 October 1797, a French balloonist named André-Jacques Garnerin rose over Paris, cut his basket loose from the balloon, and dropped. His silk canopy opened at about 910 metres. It was the first well-documented parachute descent. Early jumps like his were mostly public shows or emergency experiments.

In 1914, Tiny Broadwick had a problem. Her static line got tangled with the aircraft, so she opened her parachute by hand instead. The Smithsonian calls it the first planned freefall descent. In 1919, after the manual ripcord was developed, Leslie Irvin made an intentional freefall jump from an aeroplane.

The sport took off after the Second World War. The first World Parachuting Championships were held at Bled in 1951, and they focused on landing accuracy. Today, parachutes are also used by firefighters called smokejumpers, who drop in to reach remote wildfires.

How a Jump Actually Works

A sport jump starts with stepping out of the plane. Falling belly-down, a skydiver soon reaches terminal velocity, about 200 km/h. Diving head-down in a streamlined shape makes you fall even faster.

To open the main parachute, the jumper releases a pilot chute, a small parachute that drags the big one out. Opening happens in three stages: activation, deployment and inflation. Most modern sport parachutes are a ram-air canopy. Air rushes into openings at the front and puffs the fabric into a wing shape that glides.

Steering is simple. Pull one toggle to turn, or pull both to slow down. Just before touching down, the jumper pulls both toggles down, which cuts forward speed and descent. A 2023 review of more than 62 million civilian jumps found an injury rate of 0.044%, with most injuries happening during landing.

Learning to Fall With Style

Beginners have several paths. In a tandem jump, a student is strapped to an instructor who handles everything, from the exit to the landing. In static-line training, a line tied to the aircraft opens the student's parachute automatically. In accelerated freefall, instructors hold on to the student's harness during freefall at first.

You can also learn without leaving the ground. Vertical wind tunnels let people practise body flight, and indoor skydiving has grown into its own competitive sport.

A modern setup includes a main parachute and a backup reserve. Many rigs also carry an automatic activation device, which opens the reserve by itself in certain conditions. Before group jumps, teams often rehearse their moves on the ground, walking through the exit and each formation. This practice has a fun name: a dirt dive. Jumps take place over a drop zone, a set landing area.

Sports Played in Mid-Air

Skydiving is not one sport but many. In formation skydiving, teams of four or eight link up in freefall to build shapes, earning one point for each correct formation. A camera flyer films every jump for the judges.

Other events get even stranger. In canopy formation, jumpers link their open parachutes together, stacking them into shapes. In freeflying, people fall sitting, standing or head-down. Skysurfers once rode boards in freefall, until that event was dropped from FAI competition in 2009. Wingsuit flyers wear suits with fabric between the arms and legs, which slows their fall and speeds them forward. You need at least 200 previous skydives to try one in the United States.

Old-school skills still count. In accuracy landing, competitors aim to touch down on the exact centre of a target. And paraski combines accuracy landing with giant slalom skiing.

Jumping From the Edge of Space

Some jumps sound almost impossible. In 1960, US Air Force officer Joseph Kittinger stepped off from 31,333 metres. He fell for 4 minutes and 37 seconds and passed 965 km/h before opening his main parachute.

In 1962, Soviet parachutist Yevgeny Andreyev jumped from a balloon near Saratov and fell 24,500 metres before opening his canopy. In 2012, Felix Baumgartner jumped from 38,969.4 metres above New Mexico and reached 1,357.6 km/h. Two years later, Alan Eustace raised the height record to 41,422 metres.

Group records are just as wild. The largest official freefall formation had 400 skydivers linked together over Udon Thani, Thailand, on 8 February 2006. In 2025, 174 skydivers built a head-down formation over Illinois. As of July 2026, that one was still listed as a preliminary record claim.

10 key facts

  1. André-Jacques Garnerin made the first well-documented parachute descent from a balloon over Paris in 1797.
  2. A belly-down skydiver falls at about 200 km/h once they reach terminal velocity.
  3. The first World Parachuting Championships were held at Bled in 1951.
  4. Canopy formation was once called canopy relative work, or CRW, pronounced 'crew'.
  5. Canopy piloting courses are often set over water because the approach speeds are so high.
  6. Skysurfing, where jumpers ride a board in freefall, was retired from FAI artistic competition in 2009.
  7. Felix Baumgartner reached 1,357.6 km/h during his 2012 jump.
  8. Alan Eustace set the exit-altitude record of 41,422 metres in 2014.
  9. The official record for the largest freefall formation is 400 skydivers, set over Thailand in 2006.
  10. In speed skydiving, competitors try to reach the highest average vertical speed over part of their fall.

Key terms, decoded

automatic activation device
A small device that opens the reserve parachute by itself if certain preset conditions are detected.
dirt dive
A rehearsal on the ground where skydivers walk through their exit and formations before the real jump.
drop zone
The planned area where parachutists land. The term is also used for the skydiving centre that runs jumps there.
pilot chute
A small parachute released first, which catches the air and pulls the main parachute out.
ram-air canopy
A parachute made of two fabric layers that fill with air through openings at the front, forming a wing that can glide and be steered.
terminal velocity
The top speed a falling object reaches when air resistance pushes back as hard as gravity pulls down.
