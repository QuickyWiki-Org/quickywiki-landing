Unix

It started as a joke aimed at the operating system its own creators had just walked away from — then quietly outlasted every corporate war fought over its name. This is how a handful of programmers' side project ended up running the world's fastest supercomputers, and even Jurassic Park.

4 min read · 9 key facts · 4 terms decoded

A Name Born From a Joke

Before Unix existed, there was Multics — a massive, ambitious operating system Bell Labs was helping build in the 1960s. It kept growing more complicated, more expensive, and more delayed, until Bell Labs finally walked away from the project altogether. A handful of its programmers, including Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie, decided to build something smaller and simpler on their own, starting in 1969.

They named their creation as a joke on the very project they'd just escaped. It was first called "Unics," short for "Uniplexed Information and Computing Service" — a lighter play on Multics' "Multiplexed" name. Somewhere along the way the spelling shifted to Unix, and even Brian Kernighan, who is credited with the pun, admits nobody quite remembers how the final name stuck.

One Small Job, Done Well

Unix was built around a simple idea: instead of one giant program trying to do everything, you write lots of small programs, each doing exactly one job well. Need to search text? There's a tool for that. Need to sort it? Another tool. Need to count words? Another one still.

The trick was connecting them. Unix let programmers chain these tools together using pipes, feeding the output of one program straight into the input of the next. Programmers Brian Kernighan and Rob Pike later summed up the whole approach in one line: the power of a system comes more from the relationships among programs than from the programs themselves.

Everything on the system — including files, storage devices, and even communication between running programs — was designed to look and behave like a simple file. That single decision made Unix far easier to reason about than the tangled systems it replaced.

The Rewrite That Made It Immortal

For its first few years, Unix was written in assembly language, tied tightly to the exact processor it ran on — first a PDP-7, then a PDP-11 starting in 1970. That meant every new machine needed the whole operating system rebuilt from scratch.

In 1973, Ken Thompson and Dennis Ritchie rewrote the Unix kernel in C, a young programming language Ritchie himself was still developing at the time. Thompson had to fight the language's limitations along the way. Ritchie later called this rewrite "the most important watershed" in Unix's entire history.

The payoff was portability. Because the kernel was no longer glued to one specific chip, Unix could be moved to new hardware far faster than its rivals, and it spread to universities and companies well beyond Bell Labs.

The Wars Fought Over One Operating System

By the mid-1970s, Bell Labs started licensing Unix's source code to outside groups — $150 for a university, $20,000 for anyone else. Universities got the code and started tinkering, and one of those tinkered versions, Berkeley's BSD, grew into a rival family all its own.

Through the 1980s, competing versions multiplied fast: UNIX System III, System V, Sun's SunOS, IBM's AIX, HP's HP-UX. Each vendor added its own features, and compatibility became a genuine mess. It took until 1989's System V Release 4 to merge System V, BSD, SunOS, and Xenix back into something more unified.

Even the name itself became contested. AT&T only let companies use the trademark "UNIX" if their systems complied with strict specifications, and by the 1990s the rights had passed through Novell and on to the Open Group, which still certifies which systems can legally use the name today.

Still Quietly Running Everything

By the 1990s, Unix and its close relatives had become the default choice for serious computing — more than 90 percent of the world's 500 fastest supercomputers ran some flavor of it. It also stayed the most widely used server operating system for decades, only losing that crown around 2005.

Unix even made it to Hollywood: in 1994's Jurassic Park, Unix is the operating system running the park's control systems, a detail real programmers still enjoy pointing out.

Its influence outlived the wars fought over its name. Free descendants like Linux and the BSD family carried its ideas forward, and Dennis Ritchie himself, interviewed in 1999, called both "quite delightful" — the healthiest continuations of what he and Ken Thompson had started three decades earlier.

9 key facts

  1. Unix's name began as a pun on Multics, the sprawling operating system Bell Labs abandoned before creating Unix.
  2. Nobody, including the programmer credited with the joke, remembers exactly how "Unics" turned into "Unix."
  3. The entire Version 7 Unix system, code and two printed manuals included, fit on a single nine-track tape under 10 megabytes.
  4. In 1975, a university could license Unix's source code for 150 dollars, while any other institution paid 20,000 dollars.
  5. Dennis Ritchie called the 1973 rewrite of Unix's kernel in the C programming language the most important watershed in its history.
  6. More than 90 percent of the world's 500 fastest supercomputers ran Unix or a Unix-like system during the 1990s.
  7. Unix runs the control systems in the 1994 film Jurassic Park.
  8. Dennis Ritchie described both Linux and BSD as quite delightful descendants of the operating system he helped build.
  9. Apple's macOS is among the small number of operating systems certified today to legally use the UNIX trademark.

Key terms, decoded

assembly language
A very low-level way of writing instructions for a computer, written specifically for one type of processor chip.
kernel
The core part of an operating system that controls a computer's hardware and lets other programs run.
pipe
A way of connecting two small programs so the output of one becomes the direct input of the next, without saving anything to a file in between.
source code
The original, human-readable text of a computer program, before it is converted into a form the computer can run directly.
