Vaccine

An idea traced back a thousand years to China ended up wiping one of history's deadliest diseases off the face of the Earth. This is how we taught the body to fight in advance.

3 min read · 8 key facts · 8 terms decoded

Teaching Your Body to Win in Advance

A vaccine is essentially a training exercise for your immune system. It contains an agent that resembles a disease-causing microorganism, often a weakened or killed form of the microbe, one of its toxins, or one of its surface proteins.

The body treats this harmless stand-in as a real threat. It attacks, destroys it, and, crucially, remembers it. If the real pathogen ever shows up, the immune system recognizes the invader and destroys it before the disease can take hold.

Most vaccines are preventive, protecting against a future infection, but some are therapeutic, designed to fight a disease that has already begun, such as cancer. Either way, the verdict of the medical world is clear: vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious diseases, and its safety and effectiveness have been widely studied and verified.

An Idea Much Older Than You Think

Long before modern medicine, people had noticed that surviving a disease often meant never catching it again. In China, hints of deliberate smallpox inoculation, a practice known as variolation, appear as early as the 10th century, with recorded use by the 16th century.

The idea traveled west in 1721, when Lady Mary Wortley Montagu brought the practice from Turkey to Britain.

The true breakthrough came from an English country doctor. Edward Jenner noticed that a mild disease called cowpox seemed to protect against deadly smallpox. In 1798 he published his findings under a title that gave the field its name: Variolae vaccinae, Latin for "smallpox of the cow". In 1881, Louis Pasteur proposed extending the words vaccine and vaccination to all protective inoculations, in honor of Jenner.

The Killer That No Longer Exists

Vaccination's greatest trophy is an empty one: smallpox, one of the most contagious and deadly human diseases, is simply gone, eradicated worldwide. It was also the first disease for which a vaccine was ever produced.

Other killers have been pushed to the brink. Polio is now restricted to only parts of three countries: Afghanistan, Nigeria, and Pakistan. The numbers for measles tell the story vividly: in 1958, the United States recorded 763,094 cases and 552 deaths, while after the vaccine's introduction, cases fell to fewer than 150 per year.

Today the World Health Organization reports that licensed vaccines exist for 25 different preventable infections, and the measles vaccine alone is estimated to prevent about a million deaths every year.

Many Recipes, One Goal

Not all vaccines are built the same way. Live attenuated vaccines use weakened viruses, as with measles, mumps, rubella, and yellow fever. Inactivated vaccines use killed microorganisms, as with polio, hepatitis A, and influenza. Subunit vaccines use only a fragment of the pathogen, like the hepatitis B shot, and newer mRNA vaccines, such as the Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna shots, take a genetic approach.

Vaccines protect communities, not just individuals. When the vast majority of people are vaccinated, outbreaks struggle to spread, shielding even those who cannot be vaccinated. This effect is called herd immunity.

No vaccine is perfect: they fail to produce immunity in an estimated 2 to 10 percent of individuals, and adverse effects, when they occur at all, are generally mild, with severe reactions extremely rare. Against that stands one of the greatest life-saving records in the history of medicine.

8 key facts

  1. The word vaccine comes from the Latin name for cowpox, meaning 'smallpox of the cow'.
  2. People in China were practicing early smallpox inoculation as far back as the 10th century.
  3. Lady Mary Wortley Montagu brought inoculation from Turkey to Britain in 1721.
  4. Smallpox was the first disease with a vaccine and the first ever eradicated from the planet.
  5. The measles vaccine alone is estimated to prevent about a million deaths every year.
  6. In 1958 the United States recorded 763,094 measles cases; after the vaccine, fewer than 150 a year.
  7. Licensed vaccines now exist for 25 different preventable infections.
  8. Polio survives today in only parts of three countries: Afghanistan, Nigeria, and Pakistan.

Key terms, decoded

cowpox
A mild disease of cows that Edward Jenner used to protect people against the far deadlier smallpox.
eradication
The permanent, worldwide elimination of a disease so that no new cases occur anywhere.
herd immunity
The community-wide protection that appears when so many people are immune that a disease struggles to spread.
inoculation
Deliberately introducing material into the body to build protection against a disease.
live attenuated
Describes a vaccine made from a living microbe that has been weakened so it trains the immune system without causing serious illness.
mRNA
A molecule carrying genetic instructions. Some modern vaccines use it to teach cells to build a harmless piece of a virus that trains immunity.
pathogen
Any microorganism that causes disease, such as a virus or bacterium.
variolation
An early form of smallpox inoculation that used material from smallpox itself, practiced centuries before modern vaccines.
