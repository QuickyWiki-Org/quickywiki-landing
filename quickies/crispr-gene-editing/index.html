CRISPR Gene Editing

It is borrowed from the immune system bacteria use to fight viruses. In 2023 it became an approved medicine — and in 2019, controversially, the first gene-edited humans were born.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 16 terms decoded

Stolen From a Bacterial Immune System

CRISPR gene editing — the acronym stands for clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats — is a genetic engineering technique by which the genomes of living organisms can be modified.

The elegant part is where it came from. It is based on a simplified version of the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 antiviral defence system: an immune mechanism bacteria evolved to recognise and cut up invading viruses. Researchers did not design the tool so much as domesticate one.

It works by delivering the Cas9 nuclease into a cell, paired with a synthetic guide RNA that specifies the target. The cell's genome is then cut at the chosen location, allowing genes to be removed or added in vivo — within the living organism. Cas9 itself is derived from the bacterium *Streptococcus pyogenes*, and the guide strands, crRNA and tracrRNA, are what tell it where to cut.

Two Ways to Repair a Cut

Cutting DNA is only half the operation. What matters is how the cell repairs the break, and there are two routes with very different outcomes.

Knock-in mutations use homology-directed repair (HDR), the traditional approach to targeted editing. HDR uses similar DNA sequences to drive repair, incorporating exogenous DNA as a template — which is how you insert something specific.

Knock-out mutations happen when the double-stranded break is repaired instead by non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) or TMEJ. These pathways often produce random deletions or insertions at the repair site, disrupting or altering the gene's function. That sounds like a defect, and it is also useful: it lets researchers generate targeted random gene disruption deliberately, to find out what a gene does by breaking it.

Why This One Changed Everything

Genome editing in eukaryotic cells had been possible by various methods since the 1980s. The problem was that those methods were inefficient and impractical at any real scale.

Cas9 changed the economics. It made editing efficient and highly selective, providing a reliable way to create a break at a precise location. Researchers can now insert Cas9 and a template RNA easily, in order to silence genes or cause point mutations at specific sites — invaluable for quickly mapping which genes drive which biological processes.

The technique is considered exceptionally precise, cost-effective and efficient, with applications across new medicines, agricultural products, genetically modified organisms, and controlling pathogens and pests. Newly engineered Cas9 variants have been developed that significantly reduce off-target activity. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of the technique — while a third group led by Virginijus Šikšnys, which shared the Kavli Prize for the same discovery, was not included.

From Breakthrough to Medicine

The CRISPR-Cas9-gRNA complex was the AAAS Breakthrough of the Year in 2015, and since then it has moved from laboratory to clinic with unusual speed.

In 2023 the first drug using CRISPR gene editing — exagamglogene autotemcel, sold as Casgevy — was approved in the United Kingdom to treat sickle-cell disease and beta thalassemia. Bahrain approved it on 2 December 2023, and the US FDA followed on 8 December 2023.

The ethical line is elsewhere, and it has already been crossed. CRISPR has been investigated on non-viable human embryos since 2015, and in 2019 the first humans were born from genome-edited embryos, in the controversial He Jiankui affair. Editing a patient's own cells is one thing; editing the germline changes every descendant, and has raised serious bioethical concerns — particularly the prospect of enabling human eugenics.

10 key facts

  1. CRISPR gene editing is adapted from the immune system bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses.
  2. The Cas9 nuclease used in editing comes from the bacterium Streptococcus pyogenes.
  3. Knock-in edits use homology-directed repair, while knock-out edits rely on error-prone end-joining repair.
  4. Genome editing in eukaryotic cells was possible since the 1980s but was too inefficient for large-scale use before CRISPR.
  5. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing the technique.
  6. A third group led by Virginijus Šikšnys shared the Kavli Prize for the same discovery but was not awarded the Nobel.
  7. The CRISPR-Cas9-gRNA complex was named the AAAS Breakthrough of the Year in 2015.
  8. Casgevy, approved in the UK in 2023, was the first drug using CRISPR gene editing, treating sickle-cell disease and beta thalassemia.
  9. The US FDA approved Casgevy on 8 December 2023, days after Bahrain became the second country to approve it.
  10. The first humans born from genome-edited embryos arrived in 2019 through the controversial He Jiankui affair.

Key terms, decoded

CRISPR
A gene-editing technique that uses a guided molecular tool to cut DNA at a chosen location.
eugenics
The discredited idea of improving humanity by controlling who is allowed to reproduce or what traits are selected.
eukaryotic cell
A cell with a nucleus, of the kind found in animals, plants and fungi.
exogenous DNA
DNA introduced from outside the cell rather than produced by it.
genome
The complete set of genetic instructions inside an organism.
germline
The cells that pass genetic material to offspring, so changes to them are inherited by every descendant.
guide RNA
A short synthetic strand that directs the cutting enzyme to the exact target sequence in the genome.
homology-directed repair
A precise DNA repair route that uses a matching template to rebuild a cut strand.
in vivo
Taking place inside a living organism rather than in a test tube.
knock-in
An edit that inserts a specific new piece of DNA at a chosen location.
knock-out
An edit that disables a gene so its function can be studied or removed.
non-homologous end joining
A quicker, error-prone repair route that rejoins cut DNA ends, often adding or losing a few letters.
nuclease
An enzyme that cuts strands of DNA or RNA.
off-target activity
When a gene-editing tool cuts somewhere other than the intended site.
point mutation
A change to a single letter of the genetic code.
sickle-cell disease
An inherited blood disorder in which red blood cells become rigid and crescent-shaped, blocking blood flow.
