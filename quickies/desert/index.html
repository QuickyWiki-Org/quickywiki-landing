Desert

A desert is defined by how little rain falls, not by how hot it gets. Which is why the largest desert on Earth is Antarctica.

4 min read · 11 key facts · 9 terms decoded

It Was Never About Heat

A desert is a landscape where little precipitation falls, and the living conditions that creates produce their own distinctive ecosystems. The temperature is not part of the definition.

The threshold is usually drawn at less than 250 mm of precipitation a year. Land receiving between 250 and 500 mm counts as semi-arid, and is generally called steppe rather than desert. Extremely arid land goes at least twelve consecutive months without any precipitation at all.

Once you use that definition, the map changes shape. About one-third of Earth's land surface is arid or semi-arid, and that includes much of the polar regions, where very little precipitation falls and the snow simply never melts. These are polar deserts, or cold deserts.

Antarctica is the world's largest cold desert, made up of roughly 98% thick continental ice sheet and 2% barren rock. Some of that bare rock sits in the Dry Valleys, which almost never get snow at all.

Carved by Heat, Then Sorted by Wind

Deserts are shaped by a slow mechanical process rather than by erosion from water.

The huge swings in temperature between day and night strain the rocks until they crack and break into pieces. Rain, when it does arrive, can shatter hot rock outright. The resulting fragments and rubble are then worked over by the wind, which picks up sand and dust that can stay airborne for long stretches and sometimes builds into sand or dust storms.

Wind-blown grains striking anything solid abrade it, smoothing rocks down over time. The wind also sorts the sand, which is why the results look so orderly: level sheets of sand in some places, high billowing dunes in others.

Not every desert is sandy. Many are flat stony plains where all the fine material has been blown away, leaving a mosaic of smooth stones known as a desert pavement, after which erosion largely stops. Where water evaporates it can leave salt pans, and where underground water reaches the surface you get an oasis.

Living on Almost Nothing

Desert plants and animals are not simply tough. They are specialised in ways that look like engineering.

Plants tend to be wiry with small leaves or none at all, water-resistant outer coatings, and often spines to stop animals eating them. Some annuals take an entirely different approach: they germinate, flower and die within a few weeks of rainfall, compressing a whole life into the window when water exists. Longer-lived plants go the other way, sending roots deep enough to reach underground moisture.

Animals face two problems at once, staying cool and finding water. Many solve the first by being active at night and sheltering in shade or underground during the day's heat. They are also very efficient at conserving water, extracting most of what they need from their food and concentrating their urine.

Some go further and shut down completely, staying dormant for long periods, then becoming active and breeding rapidly when rain finally comes.

Why Deserts Sit Where They Do

Deserts are not scattered randomly. They can be classified by the weather pattern that creates them, and the biggest category explains a band you can see on any world map.

Trade wind deserts form on either side of the horse latitudes, at 30 to 35 degrees north and south, where large-scale descent of dry air happens. The Sahara is one of these. Others are mid-latitude, rain shadow, coastal, monsoon or polar deserts.

People have lived in and around them for millennia. Nomads moved flocks to wherever grazing appeared, while oases allowed a settled life. Trade routes crossed them, especially the Sahara, where camel caravans carried salt, gold and ivory, and where large numbers of enslaved people were also taken north.

Farming is possible with irrigation, and California's Imperial Valley shows barren land made productive by importing water. But cultivating semi-arid land encourages soil erosion, and is one of the causes of spreading desertification.

11 key facts

  1. A desert is defined by low precipitation, not by high temperature.
  2. The usual threshold is less than 250 millimetres of precipitation a year.
  3. About one third of Earth's land surface is arid or semi-arid.
  4. Antarctica is the world's largest cold desert, about 98 percent ice sheet and 2 percent bare rock.
  5. Antarctica's Dry Valleys almost never receive any snow.
  6. Deserts are shaped largely by temperature swings cracking rock, then wind sorting the debris.
  7. Many deserts are not sandy at all but stony plains called desert pavements, where erosion has largely stopped.
  8. Some desert annual plants complete their entire life cycle within weeks of a single rainfall.
  9. Desert animals often get most of their water from food and concentrate their urine to save the rest.
  10. Trade wind deserts, including the Sahara, form where dry air descends at 30 to 35 degrees north and south.
  11. Farming semi-arid land encourages soil erosion and is one cause of desertification.

Key terms, decoded

arid
So dry that very little can grow without additional water.
desert pavement
A desert surface of closely packed smooth stones left behind after wind removes all the fine material.
desertification
The process by which fertile land turns into desert, often worsened by farming and erosion.
dune
A hill or ridge of sand built up and shaped by the wind.
oasis
A fertile spot in a desert where underground water reaches the surface.
precipitation
Any water falling from the sky, including rain, snow, sleet and hail.
salt pan
A flat crust of salt left behind when a temporary desert lake evaporates.
steppe
Semi-arid grassland receiving more rain than a desert but not enough for forest.
trade wind deserts
Deserts formed where large masses of dry air descend, on either side of the tropics.
