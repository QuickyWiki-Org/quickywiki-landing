Ocean

It holds 97% of Earth's water, covers nearly 71% of the planet, and still supplies half the oxygen you breathe. We have named about 230,000 of its species and suspect there are two million.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 15 terms decoded

Ninety-Seven Percent of the Water

The ocean is the body of salt water covering approximately 70.8% of Earth. It is conventionally divided into named oceans — in descending order by area, the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Antarctic/Southern and Arctic — themselves divided into seas and gulfs.

It contains 97% of Earth's water and is the primary component of the planet's hydrosphere. All the rivers, lakes, ice caps, groundwater and clouds combined make up the remaining three percent.

Its role is not passive storage. The ocean acts as a huge reservoir of heat for Earth's energy budget, and for its carbon cycle and water cycle, forming the basis of climate and weather patterns worldwide. It also originated photosynthesis, and therefore Earth's atmospheric oxygen — and still supplies half of it today.

The Thin Layer Where Life Happens

Scientists divide the ocean vertically into zones defined by light, and the divisions are stark.

The photic zone starts at the surface and is defined as the depth at which light intensity is only 1% of the surface value — approximately 200 m in the open ocean. This is the only zone where photosynthesis can occur, where plants and phytoplankton use light, water, carbon dioxide and nutrients to produce organic matter.

That makes the photic zone the most biodiverse part of the ocean and the source of the food supply sustaining most of the ocean ecosystem. Light penetrates only a few hundred metres further; everything below is cold and dark, in the mesopelagic and aphotic zones. The pelagic zone — the open ocean's whole water column from surface to floor — is therefore mostly darkness with a thin lit skin on top.

Rivers Inside the Sea

Ocean temperature depends on how much solar radiation reaches the surface. In the tropics, surface temperatures can exceed 30 °C. Near the poles, where sea ice forms, equilibrium temperature is about −2 °C. But at depth, everywhere on Earth, the range narrows to between −2 °C and 5 °C.

Constant circulation creates ocean currents, driven by temperature and salinity differences, atmospheric circulation, and the Coriolis effect. Tides create tidal currents; wind and waves create surface currents.

The major currents — the Gulf Stream, Kuroshio Current, Agulhas Current and Antarctic Circumpolar Current — transport massive quantities of water, gases, pollutants and heat between parts of the world and from the surface into the deep. This is why the ocean is a climate system rather than a body of water: it moves heat around the planet on a scale nothing else matches.

Absorbing Our Carbon, at a Cost

Ocean water contains dissolved gases including oxygen, carbon dioxide and nitrogen, exchanged at the surface, with solubility depending on temperature and salinity.

That exchange has become a problem. Atmospheric carbon dioxide is rising due to emissions, mainly from fossil fuel combustion — and as the oceans absorb that CO₂, the higher concentration leads to ocean acidification, a drop in pH. The ocean is buffering the atmosphere by changing its own chemistry.

It gives humans a great deal: ecosystem services, seafood and marine resources, and transport. It is known to host over 230,000 species, and may hold considerably more — perhaps over two million. Against that, the threats stack up: marine pollution, overfishing, and the effects of climate change including ocean warming, acidification and sea level rise. Human activity is mostly coastal, and the continental shelf and coastal waters are the most affected.

10 key facts

  1. The ocean covers approximately 70.8% of Earth and contains 97% of the planet's water.
  2. It originated photosynthesis and still supplies half of Earth's atmospheric oxygen.
  3. The photic zone, where photosynthesis is possible, reaches only about 200 metres down in the open ocean.
  4. The photic zone is defined as the depth at which light falls to 1% of its surface intensity.
  5. Tropical surface water can exceed 30 °C, but deep ocean temperatures everywhere range only between −2 °C and 5 °C.
  6. Ocean currents are driven by temperature and salinity differences, wind, tides and the Coriolis effect.
  7. The Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Agulhas and Antarctic Circumpolar currents move enormous amounts of heat around the planet.
  8. As the ocean absorbs rising atmospheric carbon dioxide, its pH drops, a process called ocean acidification.
  9. Over 230,000 ocean species are known, but there may be more than two million.
  10. Human activity is mostly coastal, so the continental shelf and coastal waters suffer the greatest impact.

Key terms, decoded

aphotic zone
The deep water below where sunlight effectively reaches, permanently dark.
biodiverse
Containing a large variety of different living species.
carbon cycle
The continuous movement of carbon between the atmosphere, oceans, land and living things.
continental shelf
The shallow submerged edge of a continent before the seabed drops steeply away.
Coriolis effect
The deflection of moving air and water caused by the Earth's rotation.
ecosystem services
The benefits people get from natural systems, such as food, clean water and climate regulation.
energy budget
The balance between energy Earth receives from the Sun and energy it radiates back to space.
hydrosphere
All the water on, under and above the surface of a planet.
ocean acidification
The gradual drop in ocean pH caused by seawater absorbing carbon dioxide from the air.
ocean current
A continuous directed movement of seawater driven by wind, temperature, salinity or tides.
pelagic zone
The open water column of the ocean, away from the seabed and the shore.
pH
A scale measuring how acidic or alkaline a liquid is.
photic zone
The upper layer of water that receives enough light for photosynthesis.
phytoplankton
Microscopic drifting algae that photosynthesise and form the base of the ocean food web.
salinity
The amount of dissolved salt in water.
