Nazca lines

Enormous animals scraped into a Peruvian desert two thousand years ago, some the length of four football pitches, laid out perfectly by people who could never step back far enough to see them.

5 min read · 10 key facts · 9 terms decoded

Drawings Only the Sky Can Read

The Nazca lines are a group of geoglyphs cut into the floor of the Nazca Desert in southern Peru, created between 500 BC and 500 AD across two major phases.

The scale is the first shock. The combined length of all the lines exceeds 1,300 km, covering an area of about 50 square kilometres. Most run straight across the landscape, but more than 70 are figurative designs of animals: a hummingbird, a spider, a fish, a condor, a heron, a monkey, a lizard, a dog, a cat and a human, plus trees and flowers. The largest figures are about 370 m long, and each shape is usually drawn as a single continuous line.

Some of them are best seen from the air, at around 500 m up, though they are also visible from the surrounding foothills and other high places. They have survived largely because of where they are: the plateau's isolation and its dry, windless, stable climate have preserved them naturally for two millennia.

How You Draw Something You Cannot Stand Back From

The technique is startlingly simple. The desert floor is covered in reddish-brown pebbles coated in iron oxide. Scrape that top layer away and you expose a yellow-grey subsoil underneath, which contrasts sharply with everything around it.

That is the entire method. The trenches are only 10 to 15 cm deep, and more than half the lines are a little over 33 cm wide, ranging from about 30 cm to 1.8 m. The exposed sub-layer contains high amounts of lime, which hardens in the morning mist to form a protective crust that shields the lines from wind erosion. The desert has effectively been sealing them in as it goes.

Working out how they were made has always been easier than working out why. Archaeologists have found wooden stakes driven into the ground at the ends of some lines, supporting the theory that simple surveying tools were used, and one of those stakes was carbon-dated to establish the age of the complex.

Debunked by Someone With a Stick and Some Rope

In 1969 Erich von Däniken proposed that "ancient astronauts" had constructed the works, a claim that spread far more widely than any archaeological paper.

It was answered practically rather than academically. In the early 21st century Joe Nickell, an American investigator of paranormal claims and folk mysteries, simply reproduced the figures using only the tools and technology that would have been available to the Nazca people. With careful planning and simple technology, he showed a small team could recreate even the largest figures within days, with no aerial assistance of any kind.

*Scientific American* described his work as "remarkable in its exactness" compared with the existing lines. The point was not that the Nazca definitely used Nickell's exact method, but that the supposed impossibility at the heart of the mystery does not exist. Building them needs planning and patience, not a view from above.

The Question Nobody Has Answered

How they were made is settled enough. Why remains genuinely open, and this is the real mystery.

The most durable proposal came from Paul Kosok, the first scholar to study them in depth, who flew over the lines in 1940 to 1941 while researching ancient irrigation and realised one was shaped like a bird. He noticed lines converging on the horizon at the winter solstice, and together with archaeologist Richard Schaedel and the German mathematician Maria Reiche he proposed the figures were astronomical markers indicating where the sun and other celestial bodies rose on significant dates. Archaeologists, historians and mathematicians have all tried since. Scholars differ, but generally ascribe religious significance to the designs.

A 2019 study added an odd wrinkle. Birds are the animals most frequently depicted, and a Japanese team found that some birds previously assumed to be local species more closely resemble exotic birds from non-desert habitats, which they suspect is closely related to the purpose of the etching itself. The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994.

The Machines Are Still Finding More

Far from being a closed book, the Nazca desert is currently yielding new figures faster than at any point in its recorded history.

A Yamagata University team has conducted fieldwork since 2006 and had found roughly 100 new geoglyphs by 2012. In 2019 the university and IBM Japan announced 143 more, one of which was located using machine learning. A cat was discovered on a steep, erosion-prone hillside in 2020, which is why nobody had spotted it before. By 2022 the count of known geoglyphs stood at 358.

Then the numbers jumped. In 2024, archaeologists from Yamagata University's Nazca Institute working with IBM Research used artificial intelligence to identify 303 previously unknown geoglyphs, depicting parrots, cats, monkeys and killer whales. The AI did the searching, but people did the confirming: the field survey ran on foot from September 2022 to February 2023 and took 1,440 hours of labour, with a further 1,200 hours spent screening candidate photographs. Archaeologists believe there are still more to be found.

10 key facts

  1. The Nazca lines were created between 500 BC and 500 AD, and their combined length exceeds 1,300 kilometres.
  2. The trenches forming the lines are only 10 to 15 centimetres deep.
  3. The lines were made simply by removing reddish-brown pebbles to expose the paler subsoil beneath.
  4. Lime in the exposed subsoil hardens in the morning mist, forming a crust that has protected the lines from wind erosion.
  5. The hummingbird figure is 93 metres long, the condor 134 metres and the spider 47 metres.
  6. Each figure is usually drawn as a single continuous line.
  7. Joe Nickell recreated the largest figures using only tools available to the Nazca, disproving the ancient astronaut claim.
  8. Wooden stakes found at the ends of some lines support the theory that simple surveying tools were used.
  9. In 2024, an AI-assisted survey identified 303 previously unknown geoglyphs near the Nazca lines.
  10. That 2024 survey still required 1,440 hours of fieldwork on foot and 1,200 hours of screening candidate images.

Key terms, decoded

carbon-dated
Working out the age of once-living material by measuring how much of a particular form of carbon remains in it.
geoglyph
A large design made on the ground by moving or removing surface material, usually only fully legible from a height.
iron oxide
The reddish-brown compound, essentially rust, that coats the desert pebbles at Nazca.
lime
A whitish mineral compound that can harden when wet, forming a protective crust.
machine learning
A way of getting computers to find patterns in data by training them on examples rather than programming each rule.
plateau
A large area of relatively flat land raised above the surrounding country.
solstice
Either of the two days each year when the sun reaches its highest or lowest point in the sky at noon.
subsoil
The paler layer of earth lying beneath the surface material.
World Heritage Site
A place recognised by UNESCO as having cultural or natural importance to all of humanity.
