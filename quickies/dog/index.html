Dog

Dogs were tamed before farming, before pottery, before writing. One 14,000-year-old grave holds a sick puppy that somebody nursed through an illness it could never have survived alone.

4 min read · 9 key facts · 9 terms decoded

Tamed Before the First Farm

Dogs beat agriculture to the punch. They were the first species to undergo domestication, over 14,000 years ago, by hunter-gatherers during the Late Pleistocene, well before anybody planted a field.

Genetic studies suggest the process may have started even earlier, possibly over 25,000 years ago, and that dogs diverged from wolves somewhere between 27,000 and 40,000 years ago. The dog is a domesticated descendant of wolves, and dogs and the modern gray wolf share a common ancestor.

That long partnership left a physical mark. Because of their lengthy association with humans, dogs gained the ability to thrive on a starch-rich diet that would be nutritionally inadequate for other canids. Living beside people literally changed what dogs could digest.

The Grave That Proves the Bond

The earliest remains generally accepted as a domesticated dog were found at Bonn-Oberkassel in Germany, dated to 14,223 years ago. Contextual, isotopic evidence, genetic and morphological evidence all show this was not a local wolf.

The burial is the remarkable part. The dog was found alongside a man and a woman. All three had been sprayed with red hematite powder and buried beneath large, thick basalt blocks.

Then comes the detail that turns archaeology into something warmer. The dog had survived canine distemper as a puppy. Survival without intensive human care is unlikely, and it happened at an age before the animal could have had any practical use to its owners. Somebody nursed a sick puppy that could do nothing for them in return, over fourteen thousand years ago.

Built by Human Choice

Dogs have been bred for desired behaviours, sensory capabilities and physical attributes, which is why breeds vary so widely in shape, size and colour. A chihuahua and a mastiff are the same species, reshaped by selective breeding.

Underneath the variety, the hardware is consistent. All dogs have the same number of bones, with the exception of the tail, and powerful jaws housing around 42 teeth. Their senses of smell, hearing and sight are all well developed.

Compared with humans, dogs have a far superior sense of smell and hearing, but inferior visual acuity. That sensory profile explains the jobs they do for people: hunting, herding, pulling loads, protection, companionship, therapy, aiding disabled people, and assisting police and military work.

How Dogs Talk to Us

Dog communication runs on several channels at once: eye gaze, facial expression, vocalisation, body posture including movements of the body and limbs, and chemical communication through scents, pheromones and taste. They also mark territory by urinating on it, something they are more likely to do on entering a new environment.

The deeper point is adaptation. Over the millennia, dogs have uniquely adapted to human behaviour, and that adaptation includes being able to understand and communicate with us specifically.

That is unusual enough that the human-canine bond has become a frequent subject of study in its own right. Their influence on human society is what earned dogs the nickname "man's best friend".

A Billion Dogs, Mostly Not Pets

The global dog population is estimated at 700 million to 1 billion, spread across the world. The dog is the most popular pet in the United States, present in 34 to 40% of households.

The global distribution is not what the pet-shop image suggests. Developed countries account for roughly 20% of the world's dogs. Around 75% are estimated to live in developing countries, mainly as feral and street dogs.

Classification has been argued over for centuries. In 1758 Carl Linnaeus listed the domestic dog as *Canis familiaris* and the grey wolf as *Canis lupus* on the very next page, treating them as separate species because of the dog's upturning tail, a trait found in no other canid. The dingo's status remains disputed and is a live political issue in Australia, because treating dingoes as wild dogs makes population control easier while treating them as a separate species supports conservation.

9 key facts

  2. Genetic evidence suggests dogs diverged from wolves between 27,000 and 40,000 years ago.
  3. The Bonn-Oberkassel dog, dated to 14,223 years ago, was buried together with a man and a woman under basalt blocks.

Key terms, decoded

canid
Any member of the dog family, which includes wolves, foxes, jackals and domestic dogs.
distemper
A serious contagious viral disease of dogs that is often fatal without careful nursing.
domestication
The long process of taming and breeding a wild species over generations until it lives alongside and depends on humans.
feral
Describing an animal from a domesticated species that lives wild, without depending on people.
isotopic evidence
Clues drawn from measuring chemical variants in bone or tissue, which can reveal diet and origin.
Late Pleistocene
The final stretch of the last ice age, roughly 129,000 to 11,700 years ago.
pheromone
A chemical released by an animal that triggers a response in another animal of the same species.
selective breeding
Choosing which animals reproduce in order to strengthen particular traits in their offspring.
visual acuity
Sharpness of vision, meaning how much fine detail the eye can resolve.
