Aurora

The northern lights are the Sun crashing into our sky — charged particles painting the night green, red, and purple. In 1859 they were bright enough to read by.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 10 terms decoded

When the Sun Touches the Sky

An aurora begins 150 million kilometers away. The Sun constantly sheds a stream of charged particles — the solar wind, rushing past Earth at around 400 kilometers per second. Our planet's magnetosphere, the vast magnetic bubble that shields us, deflects most of it. But during solar disturbances, magnetic field lines snap and reconnect, hurling electrons and protons down into the upper atmosphere.

There, at altitudes of 80 kilometers and up, the particles slam into oxygen and nitrogen atoms and excite them. As the atoms relax, they release the extra energy as light — curtains, rays, spirals, and flickers dancing across the sky. In the north the display is called the aurora borealis; around the South Pole, the aurora australis. The two are mirror images, changing simultaneously on opposite ends of the Earth.

A Color Code Written in Altitude

Auroral colors are chemistry at specific heights. The classic green — the most common color — comes from atomic oxygen around 100 kilometers up. Rare, blood-red curtains at the highest altitudes are also oxygen, while blues and purples lower down come from nitrogen. Pinks and whites are mixtures.

There's a lovely quirk in the physics: oxygen's emissions are "forbidden transitions" — slow processes taking 0.7 seconds for green light and up to two minutes for red. The atom can only finish its glow if nothing bumps into it first, which is why those colors need the thin, quiet air of extreme altitude. Scientists have also catalogued a strange auroral cousin nicknamed STEVE: a narrow 25-kilometer ribbon of hot plasma at 450 kilometers altitude, blazing at 3,000°C and flowing at 6 kilometers per second.

The Night the Sky Went Electric

Auroras normally hug the polar regions — a band about 660 kilometers wide centered near 67 degrees latitude, best seen around the peak of the Sun's 11-year activity cycle. But when the Sun erupts violently, the auroral oval swells toward the equator. During the solar storms of May 2024, auroras were spotted as far south as Ferdows, Iran.

Nothing compares to the Carrington Event of 1859, the most spectacular auroral display ever recorded. In Boston, the sky at one o'clock in the morning glowed brightly enough to read ordinary print by. The accompanying geomagnetic storm surged through 125,000 miles of telegraph lines — yet some operators discovered they could disconnect their batteries entirely and keep sending messages on auroral current alone. A Japanese diary from 1770, rediscovered in 2017, hints at a storm that may have been even larger.

Not Just an Earth Thing

The name aurora comes from the Roman goddess of dawn — Galileo used "aurora borealis" in 1619 — but humans were recording the lights long before: the earliest known written account traces to roughly 2600 BC in China. Aboriginal Australian peoples associated the glow with fire, and the Māori of New Zealand called the southern lights great torches in the sky. Observers through history have even reported faint crackling sounds during strong displays — a phenomenon linked to charged particles in a cold night's atmospheric inversion layer, and still scientifically disputed.

And Earth holds no monopoly. Jupiter's auroras are fed partly by plasma from its volcanic moon Io; Saturn's are driven by the solar wind; Mars has shown a local aurora; and magnetless Venus produces diffuse patches of night-side glow. Far beyond the Solar System, astronomers found a brown dwarf whose auroras shine a million times brighter than our northern lights.

10 key facts

  1. Auroras are caused by charged particles from the Sun colliding with oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere.
  2. Green, the most common auroral color, comes from oxygen atoms about 100 kilometers up.
  3. Oxygen's red auroral glow takes up to two minutes to emit, so it survives only in the thinnest air.
  4. During the 1859 Carrington Event, the aurora over Boston was bright enough to read print by at 1 a.m.
  5. Telegraph operators in 1859 sent messages using auroral current alone, with their batteries disconnected.
  6. In May 2024, auroras were seen as far south as Ferdows, Iran.
  7. The earliest known written record of an aurora dates to about 2600 BC in China.
  8. The northern and southern auroras change simultaneously as near mirror images of each other.
  9. Jupiter's auroras are partly powered by plasma from its volcanic moon Io.
  10. A brown dwarf star has auroras a million times brighter than Earth's northern lights.

Key terms, decoded

auroral oval
The ring-shaped zone around each magnetic pole where auroras most often appear.
brown dwarf
A failed star too small to sustain the nuclear burning that powers true stars.
forbidden transition
A slow, delicate way for an atom to release light, possible only where collisions are rare.
geomagnetic storm
A major disturbance of Earth's magnetic field caused by solar eruptions, capable of disrupting power and communications.
inversion layer
A layer of atmosphere where temperature rises with height instead of falling, trapping air beneath it.
magnetic reconnection
The snapping and rejoining of magnetic field lines, which flings charged particles toward Earth's poles.
magnetosphere
The magnetic bubble around Earth that deflects most of the solar wind.
plasma
A gas so energized that its atoms have split into charged particles — the state of matter that makes up stars.
solar wind
A continuous stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun through the Solar System.
STEVE
A narrow ribbon of hot flowing plasma that glows like an aurora but forms by a different mechanism.
