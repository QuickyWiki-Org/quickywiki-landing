The Universal Declaration of Human Rights: 48 Yes, Zero No

In 1948, a war-scarred world agreed on what every human being is owed — and not a single country voted against it. One word changed by an Indian delegate still echoes today.

3 min read · 8 key facts · 7 terms decoded

A Promise After the Worst War

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) was adopted by the United Nations on 10 December 1948, setting out universal standards of dignity, freedom, and equality.

The vote was remarkable: of 58 UN members, 48 voted in favor, none against, eight abstained, and two did not vote. After the horrors of World War II, the world found rare common ground.

The People Who Wrote It

Eleanor Roosevelt chaired the drafting committee and helped win support across rival political blocs. The Canadian scholar John Peters Humphrey drafted the blueprint, while French jurist René Cassin shaped the first draft like a Greek temple, with a foundation, columns, and a pediment.

It was a genuinely global effort. China's P.C. Chang drew on Confucian ideas to break negotiation deadlocks and urged removing religious references.

One Word That Mattered

The Indian delegate Hansa Mehta pushed a crucial change: from 'all men are created equal' to 'all human beings are created equal.'

Not everyone signed on. The Soviet bloc abstained, arguing the text didn't condemn fascism strongly enough, while Saudi Arabia abstained over articles on religious conversion and equal marriage rights.

Non-Binding, But Powerful

The UDHR is a non-binding resolution — it carries no direct legal enforcement — yet it has shaped customary international law, national constitutions, and human-rights treaties worldwide.

Its reach is staggering: it exists in 577 translations, and all 193 current UN member states have ratified at least one of nine binding treaties influenced by it.
