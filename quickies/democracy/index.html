Democracy

The word first appeared in 5th-century BC Athens to mean rule of the people. As of 2022, less than half the world's population lives in one.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 14 terms decoded

Rule of the People, Minimally Defined

Democracy is a form of government in which political power is vested in the people or the population of a state. But that sentence hides an argument that has never been settled.

Under a minimalist definition, democracy simply means rulers are chosen through competitive elections. Under maximalist definitions, it additionally requires guarantees of civil liberties and human rights.

That gap is the source of most modern disputes about whether a given country qualifies. Features associated with democracy typically include freedom of assembly, association and religion, freedom of speech, personal property, citizenship, consent of the governed, voting rights, freedom from unwarranted deprivation of life and liberty, and minority rights. Whether a state that holds elections but suppresses speech counts as a democracy depends entirely on which definition you started from.

Direct, Representative, Liberal

The mechanics come in distinct forms. In a direct democracy, the people have the direct authority to deliberate and decide legislation themselves. In a representative democracy, they choose officials through elections to do it for them.

History shows plenty of direct democracy, where communities made decisions through popular assembly. Today the dominant form is representative — citizens electing officials to govern on their behalf, in parliamentary or presidential systems.

The common modern variant is liberal democracy, and its distinguishing feature is a limit on the majority. The powers of the majority are exercised within a representative framework, but a constitution and a supreme court restrain them and protect the minority — usually by securing individual rights such as freedom of speech and association for everyone. That is the crucial design choice: a democracy that cannot outvote your basic rights.

Who Counted as 'the People'

The term appeared in the 5th century BC in Greek city-states, notably Classical Athens, meaning "rule of the people" — in explicit contrast to aristocracy, "rule of an elite".

The uncomfortable history is in who qualified. In virtually all democratic governments throughout ancient and modern history, democratic citizenship was initially restricted to an elite class. The definition of "the people", and how authority is shared among or delegated by them, has changed over time and at different rates in different countries.

The expansion to all adult citizens was not a natural unfolding but a fight. In most modern democracies it was achieved through the suffrage movements of the 19th and 20th centuries. Democracy did not arrive complete and then include people; it arrived narrow, and was forced open.

Popular, Prevalent, and Disappointing

Democracy contrasts with systems where power is not vested in the general population, such as authoritarian states. Historically it was a rare and vulnerable form of government, becoming more prevalent only since the 19th century through successive waves of democratization.

It now carries considerable legitimacy: public opinion across regions tends to strongly favour democratic systems over the alternatives, and even authoritarian states go to the trouble of presenting themselves as democratic. The outcomes support the preference — democracy more consistently produces improved health, education and economic results.

And yet. According to the V-Dem Democracy indices and *The Economist* Democracy Index, less than half the world's population lives in a democracy as of 2022. Meanwhile Pew Research Center found that dissatisfaction with democratic performance is common even in many established democracies — the system is widely valued and widely disappointing at the same time.

10 key facts

  1. Minimalist definitions of democracy require only competitive elections, while maximalist ones also require civil liberties and human rights.
  2. In a direct democracy people decide legislation themselves; in a representative democracy they elect others to do it.
  3. Liberal democracy limits the majority through a constitution and supreme court in order to protect minorities.
  4. The word democracy appeared in 5th-century BC Greek city-states, notably Athens, meaning rule of the people.
  5. It was coined in contrast to aristocracy, meaning rule of an elite.
  6. In virtually all democracies through history, citizenship was initially restricted to an elite class.
  7. The extension of the vote to all adult citizens came through the suffrage movements of the 19th and 20th centuries.
  8. Democracy was historically rare and vulnerable, becoming widespread only after the 19th century.
  9. As of 2022, less than half the world's population lives in a democracy.
  10. Even authoritarian states typically try to present themselves as democratic, and dissatisfaction is common even in established democracies.

Key terms, decoded

aristocracy
Rule by a privileged elite, usually hereditary.
authoritarianism
A system concentrating power in a leader or small group, with little accountability to the population.
civil liberties
Basic freedoms protected from government interference, such as speech, assembly and religion.
consent of the governed
The principle that a government's authority is legitimate only if the people accept it.
democracy
A system of government in which political power ultimately rests with the population.
democratization
The process by which a country moves toward democratic government.
direct democracy
A system where citizens vote on laws themselves rather than electing representatives to do it.
legitimacy
The widely held belief that an authority has the right to govern.
liberal democracy
A representative system in which a constitution and courts limit majority power to protect individual rights.
parliamentary system
A system where the government is drawn from and accountable to the elected legislature.
popular assembly
A gathering of citizens who debate and decide matters collectively in person.
presidential system
A system where a separately elected president heads the government independently of the legislature.
representative democracy
A system where citizens elect officials to make decisions on their behalf.
suffrage
The right to vote in political elections.
