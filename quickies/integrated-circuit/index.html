Integrated Circuit

A sliver of silicon smaller than your fingernail can hold billions of tiny switches — and it all began with one scientist racing fragile gold wires against a rival's silicon chip, six months apart. Here's how that single invention quietly took over almost every gadget on Earth.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 18 terms decoded

Billions of Switches, One Fingernail

Hold up your finger and look at the nail. A modern chip built to fit inside that same tiny footprint can hold many billions of transistors, the tiny switches that make electronics think. They're all printed onto one flat piece of silicon, wired together without a single hand-soldered joint.

That single sliver is an integrated circuit, often just called a chip or a microchip. Before it existed, every transistor, resistor, and capacitor in a circuit was its own separate part, wired to its neighbors one at a time. Building anything complex meant building it huge.

Cramming an entire circuit onto one piece of semiconductor material did more than shrink electronics. It made them dramatically faster, cheaper, and far more reliable, since there were suddenly far fewer wires and connections left to fail.

The Six-Month Race That Changed Electronics

In July 1958, engineer Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments started sketching an idea: what if every part of a circuit came from the same block of material, instead of being wired together separately? By September 12, 1958, he had a working demo, built from germanium with fragile external gold wires holding it all together.

Kilby's chip proved the idea worked, but those gold wires made mass production impossible. About six months later, Robert Noyce at Fairchild Semiconductor solved that problem with a genuinely monolithic chip, built entirely from silicon using methods that let every connection be printed rather than hand-wired.

Both approaches mattered, but Noyce's version was the one manufacturing could actually scale. Kilby was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000 for starting it all.

Printed, Not Wired: How a Chip Gets Built

A chip isn't assembled piece by piece. It's printed. Manufacturers start with a round silicon wafer, up to 300 millimeters across, and repeat three steps over and over: photolithography to mark out a pattern, deposition to lay down new material, and etching to cut away what isn't needed.

Each round of these steps adds another paper-thin layer of transistors, insulators, or metal wiring, stacked on top of the last. As of 2022, factories were routinely printing transistors just 5 nanometers across.

None of this comes cheap. Building a single fabrication facility capable of this precision can cost more than 12 billion US dollars, which is exactly why chip factories are among the most expensive buildings on Earth.

From Ten Transistors to Five Trillion

Chips didn't start out packed. The earliest commercial integrated circuits, launched in 1964, held somewhere between one and ten transistors, a scale engineers later nicknamed small-scale integration. Four years later, that number had already climbed into the hundreds.

By 1971 chips held thousands of transistors, entering the era of large-scale integration, and eventually hundreds of thousands, an era called very-large-scale integration. In 1986, the first one-megabit memory chips arrived, each holding more than a million transistors.

Microprocessors crossed the million-transistor mark in 1989 and the billion-transistor mark in 2005. By 2023, the densest chips on the market held more than 5.3 trillion transistors, a number that would have sounded like science fiction to the engineers of 1964.

Why Nearly Everything Runs on This One Invention

The integrated circuit didn't stay a lab curiosity for long. NASA's Apollo program became the single largest buyer of integrated circuits between 1961 and 1965, betting the moon landing on a technology barely a few years old.

That early bet paid off fast. A single chip cost about 50 dollars in 1962; by 1968 the price had fallen to just 2.33 dollars, and it kept falling from there. Cheap, tiny, reliable circuits suddenly made sense for far more than rockets.

Today integrated circuits sit inside computers, smartphones, televisions, and even the appliances in a kitchen. They're smaller, faster, and less expensive than any circuit built from separate discrete components ever could be, which is exactly why almost nothing electronic is built without one.

Key terms, decoded

deposition
A manufacturing step where a very thin layer of material is added on top of a surface.
discrete component
An individual electronic part, like a single transistor or resistor, that must be wired to other parts by hand rather than printed as one unit.
etching
A manufacturing step where unwanted material is chemically dissolved away, leaving only the desired pattern behind.
fabrication facility
A specialized, extremely expensive factory built specifically to manufacture computer chips.
germanium
A chemical element that was used to build the very first integrated circuits before silicon became the standard material.
integrated circuit
A complete electronic circuit with all its parts built and connected on a single small piece of material, instead of wired together from separate pieces.
large-scale integration
A chip-making era where a single chip could contain thousands of transistors.
microchip
Another common name for an integrated circuit, a tiny slice of material holding a complete electronic circuit.
microprocessor
The main chip inside a computer that carries out calculations and runs instructions.
monolithic
Built from one single continuous piece of material rather than assembled from many separate parts.
nanometer
An extremely small unit of length equal to one billionth of a meter, used to measure the tiniest parts of a chip.
photolithography
A manufacturing technique that uses beams of light to print extremely small, precise patterns onto a surface.
semiconductor material
A material that conducts electricity better than an insulator like rubber but not as well as a metal, which makes it useful for precisely controlling electric current.
silicon
A common natural element used to build computer chips because engineers can precisely control how well it conducts electricity.
small-scale integration
An early chip-making era where a single chip contained only about one to ten transistors.
transistor
A tiny electronic switch that turns electric current on and off or boosts it, acting like a miniature gate inside an electronic device.
very-large-scale integration
A chip-making era where a single chip could contain hundreds of thousands of transistors.
wafer
A thin, round slice of silicon that serves as the base surface chips are built on top of.
