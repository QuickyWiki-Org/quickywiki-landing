Rubik's Cube

Its inventor scrambled it, then needed a month to work out how to put it back — and there are 43 quintillion ways to get it wrong, yet 20 moves is always enough to fix it.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 8 terms decoded

The Inventor Couldn't Solve It Either

Ernő Rubik was a Hungarian sculptor and professor of architecture, not a toymaker. In 1974 he built a 3D combination puzzle he called the Magic Cube, filing a Hungarian patent on 30 January 1975.

Then he scrambled it — and discovered he had trapped himself. It took him roughly a month to work out how to restore his own creation. During that month he grasped both the combinatorial complexity he had accidentally built and its potential as a game.

The engineering is the quietly clever part. An internal pivot mechanism lets each layer rotate independently while every piece stays attached to the core. There is no frame holding it together and no obvious hinge. The cube appears to be nine loose stickers per face that somehow never fall apart, which is precisely why picking one up for the first time feels faintly impossible.

Forty-Three Quintillion Ways to Be Wrong

The exact number of possible arrangements of a standard 3×3×3 cube is 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 — about 43 quintillion.

That figure resists intuition, so here is the comparison the article offers: if you had one standard-sized Rubik's Cube for every possible permutation, you could cover the Earth's entire surface 275 times over. Not the land. The whole planet, 275 layers deep.

Only one of those arrangements is solved. This is what makes the puzzle genuinely hard rather than merely fiddly — random twisting is hopeless, and no amount of luck will carry you home. The colour scheme has been standardised since 1988: white opposite yellow, blue opposite green, orange opposite red, with red, white and blue arranged clockwise in that order. On early cubes the colour positions varied from cube to cube.

Twenty Moves Is Always Enough

Here is the result that makes mathematicians smile. Despite those 43 quintillion arrangements, in July 2010 researchers proved that any configuration of a Rubik's Cube can be solved in 20 moves or fewer.

That threshold has a name: God's Number. It is not the number of moves a person will actually use — it is the worst case for a perfect solver who always chooses the optimal path. An earlier 2007 result had established that 26 moves suffice under the stricter quarter-turn metric, where a 180° twist counts as two moves rather than one.

Humans do not solve cubes that way. Popular methods trade efficiency for memorability, working in stages: CFOP (also called the Fridrich method), layer-by-layer, the Petrus method and the Roux method. A layer-by-layer solve may take fifty or more moves. It just happens to fit in a human head.

From Toy Fad to Timed Sport

Rubik licensed the puzzle to Pentangle Puzzles in the UK in 1978, then to Ideal Toy Corp for its 1980 international release, brokered by businessman Tibor Laczi and Seven Towns founder Tom Kremer.

What followed was one of the great toy manias. Around 200 million cubes sold between 1980 and 1983 alone. It won the 1980 German Game of the Year special award for Best Puzzle, and by January 2024 roughly 500 million had been sold — making it the world's bestselling puzzle game and bestselling toy. It entered the US National Toy Hall of Fame in 2014.

The fad then did something unusual: it turned into a sport. Speedcubers compete under the World Cube Association, founded in 2003, which organises competitions worldwide and ratifies records. Those records are now frankly absurd — a 2.76-second single solve by Teodor Zajder in February 2026, and a 3.51-second average by Yiheng Wang in June 2026.

Key terms, decoded

CFOP
A popular staged solving method, also called the Fridrich method, that builds a cross, then corners and edges, then finishes the last layer.
combination puzzle
A puzzle made of pieces that can be rearranged into many configurations, where the goal is to return them to one specific arrangement.
God's Number
The smallest number of moves that is always enough to solve any scrambled position of a puzzle, no matter how badly mixed.
permutation
One particular way of arranging a set of items. Changing the order of even two pieces counts as a different permutation.
pivot mechanism
The hidden core inside the cube that holds every piece in place while still letting each layer rotate freely.
quarter-turn metric
A stricter way of counting moves in which turning a face by 180 degrees counts as two moves rather than one.
quintillion
A number equal to a billion billion, written as one followed by eighteen zeros.
speedcubing
The competitive sport of solving twisty puzzles as fast as possible under timed, judged conditions.
