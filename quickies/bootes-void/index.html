Boötes Void

Astronomers pointed their instruments at a patch of sky near the constellation Boötes expecting thousands of galaxies — and found almost nothing. Here's how a void nearly 200 million light-years wide earned the nickname 'the Great Nothing.'

3 min read · 10 key facts · 6 terms decoded

A 200-Million-Light-Year Gap in the Universe

Picture flying through space for 200 million light-years and finding almost nothing along the way. That is the reality inside the Boötes Void, a roughly spherical bubble of near-empty space with a radius of 62 megaparsecs, or close to 200 million light-years across. It sits in the direction of the constellation Boötes, its center roughly 700 million light-years from Earth.

Astronomers expected a region this size to hold about 2,000 galaxies, based on how galaxies are normally spread through the universe. Instead, surveys found only 60. That is less than three percent of what the numbers predicted, which is exactly why researchers nicknamed it the Great Nothing.

Even among cosmic voids, that ratio is extreme. Most empty patches of space still hold a reasonable share of galaxies; this one barely holds any at all, making it one of the largest known voids in the visible universe.

Stumbled Onto While Mapping the Sky

The void was not something anyone went looking for. In 1981, astronomer Robert Kirshner stumbled onto it almost by accident while running a redshift survey, a project meant to map how galaxies are arranged across the universe on the largest scales.

As Kirshner and his team measured galaxy after galaxy in that patch of sky, the data kept coming back empty. Where earlier surveys had found bustling clusters, this region near Boötes returned nothing, pointed at right ascension 14h 50m and declination 46 degrees.

One small detail trips people up: Boötes is pronounced boh-OH-teez, in three syllables, not like footwear. The two dots over the second "o" tell readers to pronounce it separately from the first, a leftover from the constellation's ancient Greek name.

Sixty Galaxies Lined Up in the Dark

The 60 galaxies that do exist inside the void are not scattered randomly. They trace out a rough cylindrical shape running through the middle of the region, rather than clumping evenly throughout the sphere.

Just outside the void's edge sit the Hercules Superclusters, dense chains of galaxy clusters that mark where the emptiness ends and more typical cosmic structure resumes. The contrast is stark: on one side, almost nothing; on the other, some of the richest galaxy groupings known.

That boundary makes the Boötes Void a useful laboratory for astronomers. It shows just how uneven the distribution of matter in the universe can get, all within a single connected region of sky.

How Do You Build a Hole This Big?

How does a hole this big form in the first place? The leading explanation is surprisingly simple: smaller voids gradually merged together over billions of years, coalescing into one giant empty region.

That merger process may also explain why the void's few galaxies line up in a rough cylinder instead of spreading out evenly. As smaller pockets of emptiness combined, the remaining galaxies could have been pushed into that leftover pattern.

Importantly, none of this breaks the standard picture of cosmology. The void's existence fits comfortably within the Lambda-CDM model, the mainstream theory describing how dark matter and dark energy shaped the universe's large-scale structure over time.

10 key facts

  1. The Boötes Void is nearly 200 million light-years across but contains only 60 galaxies.
  2. Astronomers had expected around 2,000 galaxies in a region that size, meaning the void has less than three percent of the galaxies predicted.
  3. Astronomer Robert Kirshner discovered the void in 1981 while running a redshift survey to map the universe's large-scale structure.
  4. The void's center lies about 700 million light-years from Earth, near the constellation Boötes.
  5. The void is nicknamed 'the Great Nothing' because of how starkly empty it is compared to expectations.
  6. Boötes is pronounced boh-OH-teez, in three syllables, not like the word 'boots.'
  7. The Hercules Superclusters, dense chains of galaxy clusters, mark the near edge of the void.
  8. The handful of galaxies inside the void are arranged in a rough cylindrical shape rather than scattered evenly.
  9. Scientists think the void formed when several smaller voids merged together over billions of years.
  10. The void's existence does not conflict with the Lambda-CDM model, the standard scientific theory of how the universe's structure formed.

Key terms, decoded

declination
A coordinate astronomers use to describe an object's north-south position in the sky, similar to latitude on a map of Earth.
Lambda-CDM model
The leading scientific theory explaining how the universe's structure formed, combining the effects of dark energy and a type of invisible matter called cold dark matter.
large-scale structure
The overall pattern formed by galaxies and empty space across the universe, arranged into clusters, chains, and voids like a giant cosmic web.
megaparsec
A unit of distance used in astronomy equal to about 3.26 million light-years, used to measure distances between galaxies and large cosmic structures.
redshift
A stretching of light waves from an object moving away from us, which shifts the light toward the red end of the color spectrum; astronomers use it to figure out how far away and how fast galaxies are moving.
right ascension
A coordinate astronomers use to describe an object's east-west position in the sky, similar to longitude on a map of Earth.
