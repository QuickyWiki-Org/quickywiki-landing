Venice

A city built on 126 islands, linked by 472 bridges, that ran the world's first international financial centre — and now fewer than 50,000 people live in the part everyone travels to see.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 11 terms decoded

A Hundred and Twenty-Six Islands

Venice is a coastal city in northeastern Italy and the capital of the Veneto region, and its construction is genuinely improbable. It is built on a group of 126 islands, separated by expanses of open water and by canals, with parts of the city linked by 472 bridges.

Those islands sit in the shallow Venetian Lagoon, an enclosed bay between the mouths of the Po and the Piave rivers — more precisely between the Brenta and the Sile.

The name comes from the ancient Veneti people who inhabited the region by the 10th century BC. Its nicknames read like a list of everything a city can be: La Dominante, La Serenissima — "the most serene" — Queen of the Adriatic, City of Water, City of Masks, City of Bridges, The Floating City, City of Canals. Few places accumulate that many names without earning them.

The First Real Financial Centre

Venice was the capital of the Republic of Venice for almost a millennium — from 810 to 1797. Across the Middle Ages and Renaissance it was a major financial and maritime power, a staging area for the Crusades and the Battle of Lepanto, and a crucial centre of commerce in silk, grain and spice.

The strongest claim is financial. The city-state is considered to have been the first real international financial centre, emerging in the 9th century and reaching its greatest prominence in the 14th. That made Venice wealthy for most of its history.

It was also an industrial pioneer. The Venetian Arsenal is considered by several historians to be the first factory in history, and was the base of Venetian naval power. A city with no farmland and no natural defences beyond water invented modern finance and mass production instead — then used both to run an empire of territories along the Adriatic and into the Italian peninsula. Venetian sovereignty ended in 1797 at the hands of Napoleon, and the city joined the Kingdom of Italy in 1866.

Fifty Thousand People in the Postcard

Here is the fact that reframes every photograph you have seen. As of 2025, the comune of Venice has 249,466 inhabitants — but fewer than 50,000 live in the historical island city, the centro storico that the entire world pictures.

Most of the population lives on the mainland, the *terraferma*, with about 25,000 on other islands in the lagoon.

So the Venice of the imagination — the canals, the bridges, the crumbling palazzi — is home to a population smaller than a modest town, inside a metropolitan area of 2.6 million when counted together with Padua and Treviso. The most famous urban landscape on Earth is, in residential terms, nearly hollow.

Beautiful, and Under Constant Threat

Venice is known for major artistic movements, especially during the Italian Renaissance, and it played an important role in the history of instrumental and operatic music — it is the birthplace of the Baroque composers Tomaso Albinoni and Antonio Vivaldi.

The praise has never really stopped. *The Times* has described it as one of Europe's most romantic cities, and *The New York Times* called it "undoubtedly the most beautiful city built by man." The lagoon and the city within it were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, covering 70,176.4 hectares.

But the threats are structural and stacking up: overtourism, pollution, tide peaks, and cruise ships sailing too close to buildings. Because Venice and its lagoon are under constant threat, its UNESCO listing has itself been under constant examination — the rare case of a place so endangered that even its protected status is repeatedly in question.

Key terms, decoded

arsenal
A place where weapons and ships are manufactured and stored, especially for a navy.
Baroque
A richly ornamented style of art and music popular in Europe in the 17th and early 18th centuries.
canal
An artificial or adapted waterway used for travel and transport within a city.
centro storico
The historic centre of an Italian city, usually its oldest and most protected district.
city-state
A self-governing state consisting of a single city and the territory it controls.
comune
The basic unit of local government in Italy, similar to a municipality or borough.
lagoon
A shallow stretch of sea water partly separated from the open sea by a low bank of land or sand.
overtourism
When so many visitors come to a place that it damages the environment and the lives of residents.
Renaissance
The period of renewed European interest in art, learning and classical ideas, roughly the 14th to 17th centuries.
terraferma
The mainland, as distinct from islands. In Venice it names the part of the city on solid ground.
tide peak
An unusually high tide that pushes sea water up into low-lying streets and buildings.
