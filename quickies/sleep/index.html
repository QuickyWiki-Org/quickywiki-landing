Sleep

You'll spend about a third of your life unconscious — and science's best guess about why is that your brain is literally washing itself.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 9 terms decoded

The Night Shift Your Brain Runs

Sleep looks like doing nothing. It isn't. While you lie still, your brain cycles through a precise program, alternating between two very different modes: non-REM sleep and REM sleep. A full cycle takes about 90 minutes, and a good night holds four to six of them.

Early in the night, deep slow-wave sleep dominates: body temperature and heart rate fall, and the brain powers down its energy use. As morning approaches, the balance flips toward REM — a bizarre state where brain waves speed up until they resemble being awake, your eyes dart around, and your muscles are switched off in a kind of temporary paralysis. That is when most dreams happen.

A Rinse Cycle for Your Head

Why does every animal with a brain risk hours of helplessness each day? A leading answer: housekeeping. During sleep, the brain flushes out metabolic waste through the glymphatic system — a plumbing network that does for the brain what the lymphatic system does for the rest of the body.

Researchers have found that this cleansing — including the removal of amyloid, a protein linked to brain disease — may be a core purpose of sleep itself. Sleep is also when the body rebuilds: growth hormone rises during deep sleep, energy stores in the brain are restocked, and memories are filed away — early-night deep sleep helps you keep facts, while late-night REM strengthens skills.

The Clock That Runs You

You don't decide when to feel sleepy — an internal circadian clock does. A tiny region of the brain tracks light and releases the hormone melatonin at night, nudging you toward sleep when it gets dark.

This system evolved under firelight and starlight, and modern life confuses it badly. Even small amounts of light at night can suppress melatonin, and blue light — the kind screens pour out — has the strongest effect of all. Meanwhile, the pressure to sleep builds chemically: a molecule called adenosine accumulates in the brain the longer you stay awake, stacking up like a debt that only sleep can pay down. Skimp on it and the bill arrives as shortened attention, worse memory, higher anxiety, and a genuinely grouchy mood.

How Much Is Enough?

The recommended dose changes across a lifetime: newborns need 14 to 17 hours, teenagers 8 to 10, and adults 7 to 9, easing to 7 or 8 past age 65. Interestingly, sleeping about 6 to 7 hours each night correlates with longevity and heart health.

Naps have their own science. The optimal power nap runs around 10 to 20 minutes — long enough to refresh, short enough to avoid slipping into deep sleep, which takes at least 30 minutes to reach. Cultures that embrace the midday siesta may be onto something: napping has been associated with 37% lower death rates from coronary heart disease. And before artificial lighting, humans commonly slept in two separate stretches per night — the single eight-hour block is a modern invention.

9 key facts

  1. A full sleep cycle lasts about 90 minutes, and a good night contains four to six of them.
  2. During REM sleep your muscles are effectively paralyzed while your brain activity looks almost awake.
  3. Sleep flushes waste from the brain through the glymphatic system, including amyloid, a protein linked to brain disease.
  4. Blue light from screens has the strongest effect of any light on suppressing melatonin, the sleep hormone.
  5. The ideal power nap is 10 to 20 minutes, because deep sleep takes at least 30 minutes to reach.
  6. Regular midday napping has been associated with a 37% lower rate of death from coronary heart disease.
  7. Before artificial lighting, sleeping in two separate stretches per night was common practice.
  8. By age two, a child's brain has already reached about 90 percent of its adult size, with growth peaking during sleep.
  9. About 10 to 15% of adults report chronic insomnia.

Key terms, decoded

adenosine
A molecule that builds up in the brain the longer you stay awake, creating the growing pressure to sleep.
amyloid
A protein that can build up in the brain and is linked to degenerative brain disease; sleep helps clear it away.
blue light
Short-wavelength light emitted strongly by screens and daytime sky, which tells the brain to stay awake.
circadian clock
The body's internal 24-hour timer that decides when you feel alert and when you feel sleepy, guided by light.
glymphatic system
The brain's waste-drainage plumbing, which flushes out metabolic byproducts during sleep.
insomnia
A sleep disorder involving persistent difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep.
melatonin
A hormone released at night that signals to the body that it is time to sleep.
non-REM sleep
The quieter stages of sleep, including the deep slow-wave stage, when the body cools down and repairs itself.
REM sleep
The sleep stage with rapid eye movements, near-waking brain activity, and most dreams, while the body's muscles stay switched off.
