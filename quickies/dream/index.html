Dream

You dream for more than two hours every single night — and then delete nearly all of it. Science still can't say where dreams come from or what they're for.

4 min read · 9 key facts · 8 terms decoded

Two Hours a Night You Don't Remember

Every night, you spend more than two hours dreaming, in episodes lasting roughly 5 to 20 minutes each. Yet the average adult remembers only about two dreams per week. The rest simply vanish.

Why do we forget? Dream recall is extremely unreliable — though it is a skill that can be trained. According to the salience hypothesis, only content that is novel, intense, or strange sticks; ordinary dreams without strong emotion fade unless you wake up in the middle of one. Women, on average, recall dreams more often than men. And despite what cartoons suggest, dreaming isn't limited to one stage of sleep: most dreams happen during REM sleep, when brain activity looks strikingly awake, but people dream in non-REM sleep too.

What We Actually Dream About

In 1966, researchers Hall and Van de Castle analyzed 1,000 dream reports from college students and found something deflating: dreams are mostly anxious. Anxiety was the single most common emotion, and negative emotions far outnumbered positive ones. Sexual content, despite its reputation, appeared in only about 8 to 10% of dreams.

Dreams are also deeply strange in structure — researchers call them phantasmagoric, meaning places and objects continuously blend into one another. And they track our waking fears: during the COVID-19 pandemic, themes of fear, illness, and death in dreams increased two to four times compared to before. People blind from birth dream too — in sound, touch, smell, and taste instead of images.

From Temple Beds to Freud's Couch

Humans have hunted for meaning in dreams for as long as we have written anything. Dream interpretation was practiced by the ancient Sumerians and by the Babylonians in the third millennium BCE. The ancient Egyptians went further with dream incubation — sleeping on special beds in sanctuaries, hoping to receive guidance in their sleep.

Millennia later, Sigmund Freud turned dreams into the centerpiece of psychoanalysis, arguing they expressed the unconscious mind through wish fulfillment — in his view, dreams even served to protect sleep itself, letting the sleeper stay asleep rather than wake. Modern science has moved on from Freud, but the fascination never faded; the formal study of dreams even has its own name: oneirology.

Why Dream at All? Five Competing Answers

Nobody knows what dreaming is for — but the theories are wonderful:

- Learning on replay: the activation-synthesis line of research ties dreams to how the brain consolidates learning; a 2010 Harvard study found dreaming about a task correlated with improved performance on it.
- Mental cleanup: Crick and Mitchison's reverse learning theory casts dreams as a purge of mental "junk", like a computer cleanup operation.
- Threat rehearsal: Revonsuo's threat simulation hypothesis says dreams evolved as a safe practice arena for danger.
- Defending vision: a 2021 theory proposes dreams keep the brain's visual regions active overnight so other senses can't take over their territory.
- Staying flexible: Erik Hoel suggests dreams prevent the brain from overfitting to routine experience, keeping us ready for novelty.

One more mystery: in dreams, physical actions take 30 to 50% longer than in waking life. Even dream time runs slow.

Taking Control of the Dream

Some people realize mid-dream that they are dreaming — and stay inside it. This is lucid dreaming, the conscious awareness of one's state while asleep. In 1975, psychologist Keith Hearne captured the first recorded message from inside a dream: his subject, Alan Worsley, deliberately moved his eyes left and right in a pre-agreed signal, flagging to the waking world that he knew he was dreaming.

Not all dream experiences are welcome. Nightmares bring fear, horror, despair, or grief strong enough to wake the dreamer. And since all mammals experience REM sleep, it's tempting to conclude that your cat dreams too — but because a dream can only be studied through the dreamer's verbal report, animal dreaming can be inferred, never confirmed. Whatever is happening behind a sleeping dog's twitching paws remains, scientifically, a secret.

9 key facts

  1. Humans dream more than two hours per night, but adults remember only about two dreams per week on average.
  2. In a landmark study of 1,000 dream reports, anxiety was the most common emotion and negative emotions far outnumbered positive ones.
  3. People blind from birth dream in sound, touch, smell, and taste.
  4. During the COVID-19 pandemic, dream themes of fear, illness, and death increased two to four times.
  5. In 1975, a lucid dreamer sent the first message from inside a dream by moving his eyes in a pre-agreed pattern.
  6. Physical actions in dreams take 30 to 50% longer than the same actions in waking life.
  7. The ancient Egyptians slept on special sanctuary beds hoping to receive guidance in their dreams.
  8. Because dreams can only be studied through verbal reports, science cannot prove whether animals dream.
  9. The scientific study of dreams is called oneirology.

Key terms, decoded

dream incubation
The ancient practice of sleeping in a special place with the intention of receiving a meaningful dream.
lucid dreaming
A dream in which the dreamer knows they are dreaming, sometimes with the ability to steer events.
oneirology
The scientific study of dreams.
overfitting
Learning past experiences too rigidly to handle new situations — a problem dreams may help prevent.
phantasmagoric
Constantly shifting and blending, like a sequence of strange images where scenes melt into one another.
REM sleep
The stage of sleep with rapid eye movements and near-waking brain activity, where most dreaming happens.
salience hypothesis
The idea that only striking, intense, or unusual dream content gets remembered, while ordinary content fades.
wish fulfillment
Freud's idea that dreams act out desires the waking mind cannot express.
