Limonene

The smell of a fresh orange peel is mostly one molecule. The same molecule strips paint, dissolves plastic and prints 3D models.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 8 terms decoded

One Molecule, That Whole Orange Smell

Squeeze an orange peel near a candle and you get a tiny flash. Most of what you just smelled, and most of what just burned, is a single compound: limonene.

It is a slightly yellow-green liquid hydrocarbon classified as a cyclic monoterpene, and it is the major component in the fragrance and essential oil of citrus fruit peels. The name comes straight from the Italian *limone*, meaning lemon.

Its official aroma description reads like a marketing brief: "citrus, orange, fresh, sweet, peely". It gives orange peel, orange juice and other citrus fruits their characteristic smell. When factories process citrus peel waste to recover the valuable parts, limonene is typically pulled out first.

The Same Molecule, Flipped in a Mirror

Here is where it gets strange. Limonene is a chiral molecule, meaning it comes in two forms that are mirror images of each other, like a left and right hand. Most biological sources make only one of them.

The common one, d-limonene, is the form found in citrus peel. That is the commercial source, and it is extracted by two main methods: centrifugal separation and steam distillation. It smells of oranges.

The rarer mirror form, l-limonene, smells completely different. It has a piny, turpentine-like odour, and it turns up in the edible parts of plants like caraway, dill and bergamot orange. Same atoms, same formula, opposite handedness, and your nose can tell them apart instantly. That is one of chemistry's neatest party tricks.

Not Just a Citrus Thing

Limonene is far more widespread than the fruit bowl suggests. It is a major component of the aromatic scents and resins of a long list of trees, both coniferous and broadleaved.

That list includes red and silver maple, cottonwoods, aspens, sumac, spruce, several pines, Douglas fir, larches, true firs, hemlocks, cedars and juniper bush. A walk through a pine forest and a glass of orange juice share a surprising amount of chemistry.

In nature the molecule is built from geranyl pyrophosphate. Chemists also use it as a starting material: the most widely practised conversion turns limonene into carvone, the compound behind spearmint, through a three-step reaction. It is stable enough to be distilled without falling apart, though at high temperatures it cracks.

In Your Cleaner, and in Your 3D Printer

The industrial list is where limonene stops sounding like a fruit and starts sounding like a workshop. It flavours food, masks the bitter taste of alkaloids in some medicines, and scents perfume, aftershave and bath products. It also works as a botanical insecticide and goes into organic herbicides.

Because it dissolves oils and comes from a renewable source, it is used as a solvent: in hand cleansers, as an adhesive remover, for stripping oil off machine parts, as a paint stripper, and as a fragrant alternative to turpentine. Stamp collectors use limonene-containing air fresheners to lift self-adhesive stamps off envelopes.

Two uses stand out. In 3D printing, models are printed in one plastic while supports are printed in high impact polystyrene, which limonene dissolves clean away. In histology, it replaces the far more toxic xylene for clearing specimens. It is not harmless, though: it can cause contact dermatitis, it is flammable, and it is toxic to aquatic life.

9 key facts

  1. Limonene takes its name from the Italian word limone, meaning lemon.
  2. The two mirror-image forms of limonene smell different: one of oranges, the other piny and turpentine-like.
  3. The piny form occurs in caraway, dill and bergamot orange.
  4. Limonene is also a major scent component of pines, spruces, cedars, junipers, maples and aspens.
  5. It is converted industrially into carvone, the compound behind spearmint, in a three-step reaction.
  6. In 3D printing, limonene dissolves high impact polystyrene supports away from the finished model.
  7. In laboratories it replaces the much more toxic xylene for clearing tissue specimens.
  8. Stamp collectors use limonene-based air fresheners to remove self-adhesive stamps from envelope paper.
  9. Limonene is flammable and toxic to aquatic life, and can cause skin irritation on contact.

Key terms, decoded

alkaloids
Bitter-tasting natural compounds from plants, many of which are used as medicines.
carvone
The compound responsible for the smell of spearmint, made industrially from limonene.
chiral
Describing a molecule that exists in two mirror-image forms which cannot be laid on top of each other.
high impact polystyrene
A tough, cheap plastic often used for the throwaway support structures in 3D printing.
histology
The study of the fine structure of tissues under a microscope.
monoterpene
A class of fragrant compounds made by plants, built from two smaller five-carbon building blocks.
solvent
A liquid that dissolves other substances, used for cleaning, thinning or extracting.
steam distillation
A way of extracting heat-sensitive oils from plants by passing steam through them and condensing what comes off.
