Andes

One peak in the Andes reaches farther from the center of the Earth than any other spot on the planet's surface, and it is not even the tallest mountain in the range.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 5 terms decoded

A Mountain Wall Down a Whole Continent

Picture a wall of mountains running down the entire western edge of South America. That is the Andes, the longest continental mountain range in the world. It stretches about 8,900 kilometres and passes through seven countries: Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia and Venezuela.

The range is 200 to 700 kilometres wide, and its average height is about 4,000 metres. No mountain range outside Asia is higher. Tucked between its ridges is the Altiplano, the second highest plateau in the world after the Tibetan Plateau.

The name probably comes from the Quechua word anti, meaning "east". Another idea links it to anta, an older Aymara word for copper. The Spanish word cordillera, used for the whole chain, comes from cordel, meaning "rope". It fits: on a map, the Andes look like a rope laid along a continent.

The Summit Farthest From Earth's Center

The tallest peak in the Andes is Aconcagua in Argentina, at about 6,961 metres. But the most surprising summit is Chimborazo in Ecuador. Its peak is farther from the center of the Earth than any other spot on the planet's surface.

How? The Earth spins, and that spin makes it fatter around the middle. This equatorial bulge lifts Chimborazo farther from the center than any other summit, even though Aconcagua stands taller above sea level.

The Andes also hold the world's highest volcanoes. Ojos del Salado, on the border of Chile and Argentina, rises to 6,893 metres. People live remarkably high here too. La Paz, Bolivia's seat of government, is the highest capital city in the world at about 3,650 metres. Parts of its neighbouring city, El Alto, reach 4,200 metres.

Mountains Pushed Up by Moving Plates

The Andes are still being built. Off the west coast, the Nazca Plate slides beneath the South American Plate. This process, called subduction, squeezes the edge of the continent and pushes the mountains up.

The Andes sit on the Pacific Ring of Fire, so the same forces also shake the ground and feed volcanoes. Active volcanoes are spread across four separate volcanic zones, with quiet gaps between them. The 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile measured 9.5, and as of 2024 it was the strongest ever recorded on seismometers.

The climate helped too. The Andes created a dry rain shadow along the Chilean coast. Starved of sediment, the sinking plate lost its well-greased surface, and that sped up the uplift. And the story may not stop at the continent's tip. Across the 1,000-kilometre Drake Passage, the mountains of the Antarctic Peninsula appear to continue the chain.

Llamas, Condors and a Lot of Frogs

The Andes are the most important region in the world for amphibians. Almost 1,000 species live there, and roughly two thirds are endemic, found nowhere else. Add almost 600 mammal species, more than 1,700 bird species and about 30,000 kinds of plants.

On the high plains, wild vicuñas and guanacos roam. Their tame relatives, the llama and the alpaca, are kept for meat and wool and as pack animals. In mountain villages that cars can barely reach, llamas and alpacas still carry loads, though less than before.

The Andean condor is the largest bird of its kind in the Western Hemisphere. Some hummingbirds live above 4,000 metres. Lake Titicaca is home to a grebe that cannot fly and a rare water frog found only there. In the misty cloud forests, look for the spectacled bear and the mountain tapir.

Roads, Terraces and a Mountain of Silver

In the 15th century the Inca Empire spread across the central Andes. Its government built roads and aqueducts, and some of these still exist today. One famous Inca site, Machu Picchu, stayed hidden on a peak where the Andes drop toward the Amazon.

People here have farmed the steep slopes with irrigation for over 6,000 years. Terracing, cutting flat steps into hillsides, became widespread under the Incas. The potato, one of the region's most important crops, also came from the Andes.

Then there is the metal. Potosí, in today's Bolivia, was one of the main silver mines of the Spanish Empire. Its silver was so famous that the Río de la Plata and Argentina take their names from it. Today Chile and Peru are the world's top two copper producers. And Argentina, Bolivia and Chile hold the three largest lithium reserves on Earth.

10 key facts

  1. The Andes are about 8,900 kilometres long, making them the longest continental mountain range in the world.
  2. The peak of Chimborazo in Ecuador is the point on Earth's surface farthest from the planet's center.
  3. Aconcagua in Argentina is the highest peak in the Andes at about 6,961 metres.
  4. Ojos del Salado, on the Chile and Argentina border, is among the world's highest volcanoes at 6,893 metres.
  5. La Paz is the highest capital city in the world at about 3,650 metres.
  6. The 1960 Valdivia earthquake measured 9.5, the strongest ever recorded on seismometers as of 2024.
  7. Almost 1,000 amphibian species live in the Andes, and about two thirds exist nowhere else.
  8. The Río de la Plata and Argentina take their names from the silver of Potosí.
  9. Argentina, Bolivia and Chile hold the three largest lithium reserves in the world.
  10. Roads and railways across the Andes are so costly to build that much passenger travel there is done by air.

Key terms, decoded

cordillera
A long chain of mountain ranges. The word comes from the Spanish cordel, meaning rope.
endemic
Found naturally in only one region and nowhere else in the world.
equatorial bulge
The extra width of the Earth around the equator. It is caused by the planet's spin.
subduction
When one plate of the Earth's crust slides beneath another. It can push up mountains and cause earthquakes and volcanoes.
Terracing
Cutting flat steps into a hillside so crops can be grown on steep slopes.
