Money

Almost none of the money in the world was ever printed, and almost none of it is worth anything by itself. It works for exactly one reason, and it is not the one most people assume.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 11 terms decoded

Four Jobs Every Currency Has to Do

Money is any item or verifiable record that is generally accepted as payment for goods and services, and for repaying debts such as taxes, within a particular country or socio-economic context.

Notice what that definition does not say. It does not say coins. It does not say notes. It says any item or verifiable record, which is why so many different things have served as money across history.

What separates money from everything else is a set of functions. It works as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, a store of value, and sometimes a standard of deferred payment. Anything that reliably does those jobs is money, whatever it happens to be made of.

The Day Money Stopped Being Worth Anything by Itself

Historically, money was an emergent market phenomenon, and it possessed intrinsic value as a commodity. The thing used as money was itself worth something.

That is no longer true. Nearly all contemporary money systems are based on unbacked fiat money, which has no use value at all. The paper is just paper.

So where does the value come from? Social convention. Its worth is derived from a shared agreement, formalised when a government or regulatory entity declares it legal tender. That declaration is the whole foundation, and it is far less solid than a lump of gold, yet in practice it works better.

The Sentence Printed on the Dollar

Legal tender is a specific legal status, not a general compliment. It means the currency must be accepted as a form of payment within the boundaries of the country.

In the case of the United States dollar, that obligation is stated directly on the note itself: it must be accepted for all debts, public and private.

That single sentence is doing an enormous amount of work. It converts a piece of printed paper with no use value into something that everyone in a country of hundreds of millions of people will accept without hesitation, because the law guarantees that everyone else will accept it too.

Most Money Was Never Printed

A country's money supply is not the pile of cash sitting in wallets and tills. That is only the first part of it.

The money supply comprises all the currency in circulation, meaning banknotes and coins currently issued, plus, depending on which definition you use, one or more types of bank money: the balances held in checking accounts, savings accounts and other kinds of bank account.

Bank money exists as entries on the books of financial institutions. It can be converted into physical notes or used directly for cashless payment. And here is the part that surprises people: in developed countries, bank money forms by far the largest part of broad money. Most of the money around you was never printed at all.

9 key facts

  1. Money is defined as any item or verifiable record generally accepted as payment, not specifically as coins or notes.
  2. Money has four distinguishing functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value and sometimes standard of deferred payment.
  3. Historically money possessed intrinsic value as a commodity, but nearly all modern money systems use unbacked fiat money.
  4. Fiat money has no use value of its own, and its worth comes from social convention.
  5. Legal tender status means a currency must be accepted as payment within a country's boundaries.
  6. United States dollars must be accepted for all debts, public and private.
  7. A country's money supply includes both physical currency in circulation and bank money.
  8. Bank money exists only as balances on the books of financial institutions.
  9. In developed countries, bank money makes up by far the largest part of broad money.

Key terms, decoded

bank money
Money that exists as balances recorded in bank accounts rather than as physical cash.
broad money
A wide measure of the money in an economy, counting bank deposits alongside physical currency.
fiat money
Currency that has no value as a material and is not backed by a commodity, but is accepted because a government declares it valid.
intrinsic value
Worth that a thing has in itself, because of what it is made of or what it can be used for.
legal tender
A form of payment that the law requires to be accepted for settling debts within a country.
medium of exchange
Something widely accepted in trade, so that people do not have to barter goods directly against each other.
money supply
The total amount of money available in an economy at a given time.
standard of deferred payment
A commonly accepted way of settling a debt that is paid off in the future rather than immediately.
store of value
Something that holds its worth over time, so it can be saved now and spent later.
unit of account
A standard measure used to price things and keep records, so that different goods can be compared on one scale.
use value
The usefulness a thing has in its own right, separate from what it can be exchanged for.
