Lightning

Right now, lightning is hitting somewhere on Earth about 44 times every second. Each flash heats the air to around 30,000 °C, and the shock wave from that sudden heat is the thunder you hear.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 7 terms decoded

44 Flashes Every Second

Lightning is striking somewhere on Earth about 44 times every second. That adds up to nearly 1.4 billion flashes a year, and about 70 percent of them happen over land in the tropics.

Each flash lasts about half a second, but it is really a chain of much shorter strokes, each one lasting only 60 to 70 millionths of a second. In that tiny window, the air around the flash heats up to about 30,000 °C. The energy released by a single flash ranges from about 200 megajoules to 7 gigajoules.

Some places get far more than their share. Over Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela, a pattern known as Catatumbo lightning produces about 250 bolts a day, on about 297 days a year. No place on Earth sees lightning more often. Other hotspots include the mountains of the eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, Singapore, and a stretch of Central Florida nicknamed Lightning Alley.

How a Storm Charges Itself

Scientists are still working out the full details, but the basic idea is agreed on: a thunderstorm builds up electric charge by knocking bits of ice together.

In the middle of the storm, air rushes upward and the temperature sits between −15 and −25 °C. Here you find a mix of tiny water droplets that stay liquid below freezing, small ice crystals, and soft hail called graupel. The rising air carries the light ice crystals up, while the heavier graupel hangs in the middle of the cloud or falls.

As they pass each other, they collide. The ice crystals pick up a positive charge and the graupel picks up a negative one. The crystals are carried to the top, so the top of the cloud becomes positive while the middle and lower parts become negative.

When a strike to the ground begins, a stepped leader works its way down from the cloud. A streamer rises from the ground to meet it, and the moment they connect, the flash races through.

The Thunder Trick

Thunder is what all that heat sounds like. The flash heats the air so fast that the gas expands outward in a shock wave, and that is the boom you hear.

The long rumble comes from the length of the bolt. Sound starts from every point along the channel, so the parts farther from you arrive a little later. That stretches one crack into a rolling growl.

Because light travels so much faster than sound, you can work out roughly how far away a strike is. Count the seconds between the flash and the thunder. A three second gap means the lightning is about 1 kilometre away, and five seconds means roughly 1.6 kilometres, or a mile. If the thunder comes almost instantly, the strike was very close, and you might even smell ozone.

You can also see lightning without hearing it. A storm can be seen from more than 160 kilometres away, but its thunder only carries about 32 kilometres.

Megaflashes and Lightning on Other Worlds

Most bolts are short, but a few are enormous. An unusually long flash is called a megaflash, one that stretches about 100 kilometres or more across the sky.

In 2025, scientists found that a flash in October 2017 had run 829 kilometres across Texas and Kansas, touching the ground in five states. Another flash, over Uruguay and northern Argentina in June 2020, lasted 17.1 seconds.

Lightning is not just an Earth thing, either. It has been seen on Jupiter and Saturn, and probably happens on Uranus and Neptune too. Lightning on Jupiter is far more powerful than ours. In 2025, researchers reported a possible sign of lightning on Mars, picked up by a microphone on NASA's Perseverance rover.

Back home, lightning even does useful work. It turns nitrogen in the air into nitrates, which rain carries down to help plants grow. And although it can be deadly, about 90 percent of people struck by lightning survive.

10 key facts

  1. Lightning strikes somewhere on Earth about 44 times every second, adding up to nearly 1.4 billion flashes a year.
  2. The air around a lightning flash heats up to about 30,000 °C.
  3. Lake Maracaibo in Venezuela gets about 250 lightning bolts a day, on about 297 days of the year.
  4. Only about 25% of lightning flashes worldwide actually reach the ground.
  5. The longest flash ever observed ran 829 km across Texas and Kansas in October 2017.
  6. A three second gap between the flash and the thunder means the strike is about 1 km away.
  7. About 90 percent of people struck by lightning survive.
  8. Passengers on a plane struck by lightning usually do not even notice, because the metal body acts as a Faraday cage.
  9. When lightning passes through sandy soil, it can melt the soil into tube-shaped structures called fulgurites.
  10. Lightning struck the Apollo 12 rocket shortly after takeoff, using its exhaust plume as a path.

Key terms, decoded

Faraday cage
A metal shell that carries electric charge around its outside, keeping whatever is inside safe.
fulgurite
A tube-shaped structure formed when lightning melts the soil it passes through.
graupel
Soft, crumbly pellets of ice, a kind of small soft hail, that form inside storm clouds.
megaflash
A single lightning flash that stretches roughly 100 kilometres or more sideways across the sky.
ozone
A form of oxygen gas made of three oxygen atoms, with a sharp, clean smell.
stepped leader
A faint channel of charged air that works its way down from a cloud in short jumps, opening the path a lightning strike will follow.
streamer
A channel of charge that rises from the ground or a tall object to meet a leader coming down from a cloud.
