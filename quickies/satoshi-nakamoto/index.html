Satoshi Nakamoto

They invented bitcoin, wrote the white paper, built the first blockchain — then vanished in 2011. The wallet has not moved since 2010 and is worth around $135 billion.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 9 terms decoded

A Name, Not a Person

Satoshi Nakamoto is the name used by the presumed pseudonymous person — or persons — who developed bitcoin, authored the bitcoin white paper, and created and deployed bitcoin's original reference implementation.

The careful wording is the point. The record does not say who Satoshi Nakamoto was; it says what the name did. As part of the implementation, Nakamoto also devised the first blockchain database, the underlying structure that everything built on the idea since has depended on.

The active window is remarkably narrow. Nakamoto is recorded as active from 31 October 2008 to 26 April 2011, and was involved in bitcoin's development until December 2010. Roughly two and a half years of public activity, and then nothing at all.

Japanese Name, British Suspicions

Nakamoto's true identity is unknown. Various people have been posited as the individual or group behind the name, and none has been established.

The available clues conflict. He used a Japanese name and gave his residence as Japan. But many have speculated that he is actually a British software and cryptography expert who worked on bitcoin in the United Kingdom.

The suspicion is not baseless — it rests on details that leak through anonymity regardless of what a profile says: the timing of activity, the idioms and spelling conventions in the writing, and the technical culture the work belongs to. A pseudonym can hide a name easily. Hiding a time zone and a dialect turns out to be much harder.

The Wallet That Never Moved

Here is the fact that keeps the mystery alive far more effectively than the identity question does.

If Nakamoto is an individual person, then his bitcoin holdings make him one of the wealthiest people in the world. The wallet holds an estimated 1.1 million bitcoins — and it has been untouched since 2010.

As of 14 July 2025, the price was more than $123,000 each, which would put the contents of that wallet at nearly $135 billion.

Sit with the restraint that represents. A fortune of that scale, publicly visible on a ledger anyone can inspect, reachable by whoever holds the keys, and not one coin has moved in over fifteen years. Every plausible explanation — lost keys, death, deliberate abstention — is remarkable in a different way.

Why the Silence Matters

The disappearance is not merely a good story. It is arguably load-bearing for the thing that was built.

A currency with a known founder has a person who can be pressured, prosecuted, discredited or simply believed. It has a centre. Nakamoto's withdrawal in December 2010 removed that centre deliberately, at the exact moment the project was becoming significant.

And the untouched wallet reinforces it. A founder holding 1.1 million coins who never sells cannot be accused of building the system to enrich himself, and cannot crash the market by cashing out. The most consequential decisions attributed to this name may not be the technical ones in the white paper at all, but the two acts of abstention that followed: leaving, and never spending.

  2. The name is credited with developing bitcoin, writing its white paper and deploying its original reference implementation.
  3. Nakamoto also devised the first blockchain database as part of that implementation.
  4. Recorded activity under the name runs only from 31 October 2008 to 26 April 2011.
  5. Nakamoto was involved in bitcoin's development until December 2010, then withdrew.
  6. He used a Japanese name and gave Japan as his residence, but many suspect a British software and cryptography expert.

Key terms, decoded

bitcoin
A digital currency that runs without any bank or government, maintained by a network of computers.
blockchain
A shared record of transactions stored as a chain of linked blocks that is very hard to alter after the fact.
cryptography
The science of encoding information so only intended parties can read or verify it.
keys
The secret codes that prove ownership and allow digital currency to be spent.
ledger
A record of transactions; in bitcoin's case one that anyone can inspect.
pseudonymous
Operating under an invented name rather than a real one, while still being a consistent identity.
reference implementation
The original working version of a system, which others treat as the definition of how it should behave.
wallet
The stored keys that control a particular holding of a digital currency.
white paper
A document laying out the design and reasoning behind a proposed system.
