Statue of Liberty

When the Statue of Liberty was unveiled in 1886, it was a shiny reddish-brown. Within twenty years it had turned green, and the public pushed back so hard against painting it that officials left it that way.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 6 terms decoded

A Gift That Came in Crates

The Statue of Liberty was a gift to the United States from the people of France. The idea is linked to Édouard de Laboulaye, a French political thinker and opponent of slavery, and it was designed by the sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi.

Bartholdi chose a small island in New York Harbor for the site, because every ship arriving in New York had to sail past it. The statue shows a robed woman inspired by Libertas, the Roman goddess of liberty. She holds a torch high, and in her other hand carries a tablet marked with the date of the US Declaration of Independence in Roman numerals.

At her feet lies a broken chain, half-hidden by her robes and hard to see from the ground. It marks the end of slavery after the American Civil War.

The statue was built in France, taken apart, packed into crates and shipped across the Atlantic. When it arrived in June 1885, 200,000 people lined the docks to welcome it.

Eiffel's Hidden Skeleton

The outside of the statue is a thin skin of copper, only 2.4 millimetres thick. Craftsmen shaped it using repoussé, heating copper sheets and beating them into shape with wooden hammers. That kept the statue light for its huge size.

Holding it all up was the job of Gustave Eiffel, the engineer who would later build the Eiffel Tower. His team designed an iron tower to stand inside the statue. He chose not to make it completely stiff. Instead, the copper skin was loosely attached with flexible iron bars, so the statue could sway slightly in the harbour winds and expand on hot days without cracking.

This made it one of the earliest examples of curtain wall construction, where the outer surface carries no weight and an inner frame does all the work.

The first rivet was driven into the statue's big toe. Once the statue was built up to its waist, Bartholdi invited reporters to have lunch on a platform inside it. The finished figure stands just over 46 metres tall.

Pennies From New Yorkers

France paid for the statue, but Americans had to pay for the huge base it would stand on, the pedestal. That turned out to be hard. Newspapers mocked the idea, the governor of New York vetoed a bill to fund it, and Congress said no as well. By 1885, the New York committee had just 3,000 dollars left and stopped work.

The publisher Joseph Pulitzer stepped in. His newspaper, the New York World, launched a drive to raise 100,000 dollars, and he promised to print the name of every donor, however small the gift. The notes poured in. A group of children sent a dollar they had saved to go to the circus. A kindergarten class in Iowa mailed in 1.35 dollars.

In five months the paper raised 102,000 dollars from 120,000 donors, with 80 percent of the money arriving in amounts of less than a dollar.

As part of the fundraising, the poet Emma Lazarus wrote The New Colossus, with its famous lines about the tired and the poor yearning to breathe free.

The Statue That Turned Green

When the statue was dedicated on 28 October 1886, New York celebrated with a parade. As it passed the Stock Exchange, traders threw ticker tape from the windows, starting the city's tradition of the ticker-tape parade.

The torch was a letdown. On the first night it gave only a faint glow, and a newspaper said it looked more like a glowworm than a beacon. The statue was run as a lighthouse, but it proved useless and was handed over to the War Department in 1901.

The biggest change came slowly. The copper started out shiny reddish-brown, but soon after 1900 a green layer called verdigris began to spread, and by 1906 it covered the whole statue. Congress planned to paint it. The public protested, and engineers found the green layer actually protected the copper. In the end, only the inside was painted.

For millions of immigrants arriving by sea, the statue became a sign of welcome. In 1924 it was declared a national monument.

10 key facts

  1. The Statue of Liberty was a gift to the United States from the people of France.
  2. The statue's copper skin is only about 2.4 mm thick.
  3. Gustave Eiffel, later famous for the Eiffel Tower, designed the statue's inner iron frame.
  4. The statue was built in France, then taken apart and shipped to New York in crates.
  5. A broken chain lies at the statue's feet, marking the end of slavery in the United States.
  6. 120,000 people donated to finish the pedestal, with most of the money coming in gifts of less than a dollar.
  7. The statue's dedication in 1886 started New York's tradition of ticker-tape parades.
  8. The statue was reddish-brown when built and had turned completely green by 1906.
  9. Public protest stopped a plan to paint the outside of the statue.
  10. The statue was run as a lighthouse until 1901, but its torch was too dim to be useful.

Key terms, decoded

curtain wall
A building method where the outer skin carries no weight and is held up by a frame inside.
pedestal
The base that a statue or column stands on.
repoussé
A way of shaping metal by hammering thin sheets into form.
rivet
A short metal pin used to fasten pieces of metal together.
ticker tape
Long, thin strips of paper once used to print stock prices, later thrown from windows in celebrations.
verdigris
A green layer that forms on copper over time as it reacts with air and water.
