Soap

Soap does not poison germs. It physically tears them apart, and the recipe for it is the oldest chemical reaction anyone ever wrote down.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 10 terms decoded

Not Poison. Demolition.

Most people assume soap is a mild disinfectant that kills germs chemically. What actually happens is closer to structural collapse.

Soap is a surfactant. When you lather it with a little water, its molecules pull apart a microorganism's outer lipid bilayer, the fatty envelope holding it together, and denature its proteins. The germ does not get poisoned. It comes apart.

The second job is hauling the wreckage away. Soap molecules have a water-loving head and a fat-loving tail, so they cluster into tiny spheres called micelles, with the water-attracting groups facing outward and a fat-attracting pocket hidden inside. Grease and grime get trapped in those pockets, shielded from the surrounding water, which makes the whole package soluble. Anything soluble goes down the drain with the rinse. This is also why scrubbing time matters more than any spray: the mechanism is mechanical.

The Oldest Written Chemistry

Soap-like materials go back roughly 4,800 years. The earliest recorded evidence of producing them dates to around 2800 BC in ancient Babylon, and proto-soaps are mentioned in Sumerian, Babylonian and Egyptian texts.

A formula was written on a Sumerian clay tablet around 2500 BC: heat a mixture of oil and wood ash. That tablet is the earliest recorded chemical reaction of any kind, and the product was used for washing woollen clothing rather than people.

Egypt had its own version. The Ebers papyrus of 1550 BC indicates that Egyptians combined animal fats or vegetable oils with a soda ash substance called trona, using the result as a medicine as well as in preparing wool for weaving. From the reign of Nabonidus in the 6th century BC comes a recipe of ashes, cypress oil and sesame oil, recorded specifically "for washing the stones for the servant girls".

The Romans Scraped Instead

For a civilisation famous for its baths, Rome was oddly late to soap. The Romans did not use it until at least the first century AD.

Their preferred method of cleaning the body was to massage oil into the skin and then scrape it off, along with the dirt, using a strigil: a curved metal blade with a handle. Effective, but not soap.

When soap does appear in Roman writing, it arrives as a foreign import. Pliny the Elder describes it as "an invention of the Gauls", and records its manufacture from tallow and ashes, noting it was used to treat sores and, among the Gauls, as a dye to redden hair. The Latin word *sapo* has long been linked to a mythical Mount Sapo where animals were sacrificed, but in all likelihood it was simply borrowed from an early Germanic language. The Romans avoided harsh soaps until they encountered the milder Gaulish ones around 58 BC.

What Is Actually in the Bar

Making toilet soap means saponification: treating fats or oils, chemically known as triglycerides, with an alkaline solution, usually lye. The fats break into salts of fatty acids, and glycerol is set free. Leave the glycerol in and the soap is softer and more moisturising; take it out and you can sell it separately.

The alkali decides the texture. Sodium hydroxide gives firm bar soaps, potassium hydroxide gives soft or liquid ones, and lithium soaps come out hard enough that they are used almost exclusively in industrial greases rather than on skin.

The fat decides the feel. Coconut, olive, palm and tallow each carry different fatty acids, so each produces a distinctly different soap. Soap made from pure olive oil, known as Castile or Marseille soap, is prized for mildness. Most body soaps are also "superfatted", carrying extra fat that guarantees no leftover lye, stops bars cracking, and replaces the skin oils that the washing just stripped away.

10 key facts

  1. A Sumerian clay tablet from around 2500 BC records a soap recipe, making it the earliest written record of any chemical reaction.
  2. Soap does not poison microorganisms, it disorganises their outer membrane and denatures their proteins so they fall apart.
  3. The earliest evidence of soap-like production dates to around 2800 BC in ancient Babylon.
  4. The Romans did not use soap until at least the first century AD, preferring to oil the skin and scrape it off with a curved metal blade.
  5. Pliny the Elder described soap as an invention of the Gauls, who also used it as a dye to redden hair.
  6. The earliest known soap was used for washing woollen clothing, not for washing people.
  7. Sodium makes firm bar soap, potassium makes soft or liquid soap, and lithium soap is hard enough that it is used mainly in machine greases.
  8. Soap made from pure olive oil is known as Castile or Marseille soap and is prized for being unusually mild.
  9. In hard water soap does not lather well and instead forms soap scum.
  10. Superfatted soaps contain deliberate excess fat, which guarantees no leftover lye remains in the bar.

Key terms, decoded

denature
To damage a protein's shape so badly that it stops working.
glycerol
A thick, sweet-tasting liquid released when fats are turned into soap, often kept in as a softener. Also called glycerin.
lipid bilayer
The double layer of fatty molecules that forms the outer skin of a cell and holds its contents in.
lye
A strong alkaline solution, such as sodium or potassium hydroxide, used to turn fats into soap.
micelle
A tiny ball of soap molecules that traps grease in its centre while presenting a water-friendly outside, so the whole thing washes away.
saponification
The chemical reaction in which fats or oils are treated with a strong alkali to produce soap.
strigil
A curved metal blade with a handle, used in the ancient world to scrape oil and dirt off the skin.
surfactant
A substance that lowers the tension between water and oil, letting them mix instead of separating.
tallow
Rendered animal fat, historically the most widely available raw material for soap.
triglyceride
The chemical name for common fats and oils, made of fatty acids joined to glycerin.
