Coffee

It started with Sufi mystics in 15th-century Yemen. It is now a $495 billion industry — and the people who grow it disproportionately live in poverty.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 12 terms decoded

A Seed, Not a Bean

Coffee is a beverage brewed from roasted, ground coffee beans. Dark-coloured and bitter, it has a stimulating effect on humans due to its caffeine content — though decaffeinated coffee is also commercially available, alongside various coffee substitutes.

The "beans" are not beans. Production begins when the seeds are separated from coffee cherries, the fruits of the Coffea plant, producing unroasted green coffee beans. Those are roasted, then ground into fine particles.

Brewing is simply steeping those grounds in hot water before filtering them out. Everything after that is presentation: espresso, French press, caffè latte, canned coffee, hot or iced, with sugar, substitutes, milk or cream added to change the flavour. One agricultural seed, one extraction method, and an enormous amount of ritual built on top.

From Sufi Yemen to the World

Though coffee is now a global commodity, its history is tied to food traditions around the Red Sea.

The earliest reports of coffee drinking concern the plant's use among the Sufis of Yemen, in southern Arabia, in the middle of the 15th century. Its first documented role was in religious practice — mystics using it to stay awake through night-long devotions.

Yemen kept the trade for two centuries: up to the end of the 17th century, most of the world's coffee was imported from Yemen. As popularity grew, cultivation spread — to Java in the 17th century, and to the Americas from the 18th. That expansion is why coffee is now grown far from where it was first drunk, and why the countries that dominate production today had no historical connection to it at all.

Two Species, Seventy Countries

Nearly all the coffee in the world comes from two bean types: *C. arabica* and *C. robusta*.

Coffee plants are cultivated in over 70 countries, primarily in the equatorial regions of the Americas, Southeast Asia, the Indian subcontinent and Africa — a belt around the middle of the planet where the altitude and climate suit the plant.

Green, unroasted coffee is traded as an agricultural commodity, priced on global markets rather than by the people who grew it. In 2023, Brazil was the leading grower, producing 31% of the world's total, followed by Vietnam. Nearly a third of all coffee on Earth comes from a single country.

Half a Trillion Dollars, and Poor Farmers

The global coffee industry was worth US$495.50 billion in 2023.

Set directly against that figure is the uncomfortable one. While coffee sales reach billions of dollars annually worldwide, coffee farmers disproportionately live in poverty. The value accumulates overwhelmingly in roasting, branding and retail — the stages furthest from the plant.

The environmental record draws criticism too. Critics of the industry point to its negative impact on the environment, including clearing of land for coffee growing and water use. Both problems share a cause: demand at half-a-trillion-dollar scale has to be met by land and water somewhere, and that somewhere is the equatorial belt, in the same countries whose growers see least of the money.

Key terms, decoded

arabica
The more delicate and widely grown coffee species, valued for its flavour.
caffeine
A natural stimulant found in coffee, tea and cocoa that increases alertness.
coffee cherry
The small red fruit of the coffee plant, whose seeds become coffee beans.
commodity
A raw material traded in bulk on global markets, priced by the market rather than the producer.
decaffeinated
Having had most of the caffeine removed.
equatorial region
The band of the world close to the equator, where coffee grows best.
espresso
Coffee made by forcing hot water through finely ground beans under pressure.
French press
A brewing method in which grounds steep loose in hot water and are then pressed down by a mesh plunger.
green coffee bean
The raw unroasted seed, which has little of the flavour or aroma of roasted coffee.
roasting
Heating green beans until chemical reactions develop the colour, aroma and flavour of coffee.
robusta
A hardier coffee species with more caffeine and a stronger, harsher taste.
steeping
Soaking ground coffee in hot water long enough to extract its flavour.
