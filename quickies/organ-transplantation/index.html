Organ Transplantation

The first organ removal for transplant happened in 1954. The United States passed one million cumulative transplants in 2022 — and still meets only a fraction of the need.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 15 terms decoded

Moving a Working Part Between People

Transplantation is a medical procedure in which an organ, tissue or cells are removed from one organism and placed in a recipient, usually to replace a damaged or missing function. Donor and recipient may be in the same place, or organs may be transported from one site to another.

The vocabulary distinguishes cases. Transplants moved within the same person's body are autografts. Transplants between two individuals of the same species are allografts, and can come from a living or cadaveric source.

The list of organs successfully transplanted is longer than most people assume: heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, pancreas, intestine, thymus and uterus. Tissues include bones, tendons, corneae, skin, heart valves, nerves and veins. Worldwide, kidneys remain the most commonly transplanted organ, followed by liver and heart. J. Hartwell Harrison performed the first organ removal for transplant in 1954, as part of the first kidney transplant.

The Tissue Nobody Counts

Organ transplants get the attention, but they are not the bulk of the work.

Cornea and musculoskeletal grafts are the most commonly transplanted tissues, and they outnumber organ transplants by more than tenfold. For every dramatic heart transplant there are many quieter operations restoring sight or repairing bone.

The logistics differ sharply too. Organ donors may be living, or deceased through brain death or circulatory death, and tissues can be recovered from donors within 24 hours after cardiac arrest. Crucially, unlike organs, most tissues — corneas excepted — can be preserved and "banked" for up to five years. An organ is a race against the clock; a tissue can wait in storage for half a decade until it is needed.

When the Body Refuses the Gift

Transplantation medicine is described as one of the most challenging and complex areas of modern medicine, and the central difficulty is biological rather than surgical.

The problem is transplant rejection: the recipient's immune system mounts a response against the transplanted organ, which can cause the transplant to fail and force immediate removal. The body treats a life-saving organ as an intruder, because that is precisely what its defences are built to detect.

Two strategies reduce the risk. Serotyping identifies the most appropriate donor-recipient match in advance, and immunosuppressant drugs dampen the immune response afterwards. Both involve trade-offs — a suppressed immune system is also a weakened one — which is why matching well beforehand matters so much.

A Million Transplants, and Not Nearly Enough

As of 9 September 2022, the United States reached one million cumulative transplants, according to the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network.

Set against demand, that milestone looks different. Global analyses indicate available transplants meet only a minority of worldwide need, with estimates that only 10–20% of need is met, plus significant inequities in access.

Scarcity of that severity generates hard ethics. The field raises questions about the definition of death, when and how consent should be given, and payment for organs — along with transplantation tourism, the socio-economic context of organ procurement, and the particular problem of organ trafficking. There is also the quieter ethical duty of not holding out false hope to patients. Recent advances offer some relief: xenotransplantation trials using genetically modified pig organs have produced early safety and feasibility data alongside real infectious and immunologic risks, while machine perfusion and normothermic ex-vivo perfusion have extended how long an organ can be preserved and assessed before surgery.

Key terms, decoded

allograft
A transplant between two different individuals of the same species.
autograft
A transplant of tissue moved from one place to another within the same person's body.
banking
Preserving and storing donated tissue so it can be used months or years later.
brain death
The irreversible loss of all brain function, legally recognised as death in many countries.
cadaveric donor
A donor who has died, whose organs or tissues are recovered for transplant.
circulatory death
Death determined by the permanent stopping of the heart and circulation.
cornea
The clear front layer of the eye that light passes through.
immunosuppressant
A drug that damps down the immune system so it does not attack a transplanted organ.
machine perfusion
Keeping a donated organ supplied with fluid by machine outside the body to preserve and assess it.
musculoskeletal graft
Transplanted bone or tendon used to repair damaged bones and joints.
organ trafficking
The illegal trade in human organs, often exploiting poor or vulnerable donors.
serotyping
Testing blood and tissue markers to find how well a donor and recipient match.
transplant rejection
The immune system attacking a transplanted organ because it recognises it as foreign.
transplantation
Moving an organ, tissue or cells from one body into another to replace a lost or damaged function.
xenotransplantation
Transplanting organs or tissue from one species into another, such as pig organs into humans.
