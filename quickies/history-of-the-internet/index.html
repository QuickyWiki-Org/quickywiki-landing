History of the Internet

In 1993 the internet carried just 1% of the world's two-way telecommunications traffic. By 2007, that number had rocketed past 97%. Here's how a Cold War-era thought experiment turned into the network that swallowed the planet's conversations.

4 min read · 8 key facts · 12 terms decoded

The Internet Started as a Cold War Thought Experiment

Long before smartphones, computer scientists dreamed of linking machines together. In the late 1950s, J. C. R. Licklider pictured a universal network where anyone could reach any computer from anywhere. He worked at the Pentagon's Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA), the same office that would later fund the internet's earliest ancestor.

The real breakthrough was an idea called packet switching. Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation proposed a distributed network built from small message blocks in the early 1960s. Around the same time, Donald Davies at Britain's National Physical Laboratory invented the same idea independently, and even proposed a national UK data network. Neither scientist knew the other was working on it.

One Government Contract Built the First Network

In 1969, ARPA signed contracts to build ARPANET, directed by Robert Taylor and managed by Lawrence Roberts. The project adopted Davies's packet-switching idea and even sought input from Baran himself. Machines called Interface Message Processors, built by a team at Bolt, Beranek, and Newman, formed the network's backbone.

The rules for how computers would actually talk to each other weren't written by generals or executives. They were mostly worked out by graduate students, led by Steve Crocker at UCLA alongside Jon Postel. Within a few years, ARPANET had grown beyond the United States, reaching the United Kingdom and Norway.

Stitching Separate Networks Into One

By the 1970s, several different packet-switched networks existed, and none of them could talk to each other. Researchers Louis Pouzin and Hubert Zimmermann at France's IRIA lab pioneered a simpler way to connect networks together. Peter Kirstein put the approach into practice at University College London in 1973, while Bob Metcalfe worked out the theory behind Ethernet.

The real glue arrived in 1974, when Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published a paper describing what became TCP/IP, the two protocols that still run the internet today. Their design borrowed ideas from France's CYCLADES project. Meanwhile, Leonard Kleinrock at UCLA supplied the mathematics proving these packet networks could actually work at scale.

From Military Project to Public Utility

Through the late 1970s, national data networks sprang up using a protocol called X.25. In the United States, the National Science Foundation funded supercomputing centers at universities, then linked them together in 1986 with NSFNET. That project, plus the new Domain Name System and wider adoption of TCP/IP, is really where the internet as we'd recognize it began.

Commercial internet service providers appeared in the US and Australia in 1989, letting ordinary companies buy a connection for the first time. But the government-run NSFNET backbone kept a lid on commercial traffic until it was finally decommissioned in 1995, handing the network over to companies like Sprint, MCI, and AT&T.

A Scientist in Switzerland Adds the Web

The internet already existed for two decades before most people could easily use it. That changed when British computer scientist Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN in Switzerland, built the World Wide Web in 1989-90. It let anyone link documents together and jump between them from any connected computer, using nothing more than a click.

The web arrived just as fiber-optic cables and a technology called wave division multiplexing were dramatically expanding how much data networks could carry. Together, they unlocked email, instant messaging, internet phone calls, video chat, blogs, social networking, and online shopping, reshaping culture and commerce almost overnight. What had been a tool for scientists and the military quickly became something ordinary households wanted in their own homes.

How the Internet Swallowed Global Communication

The internet's takeover of how humans communicate happened astonishingly fast. In 1993, it carried just 1% of all information flowing through the world's two-way telecommunications networks. By 2000, that figure had rocketed to 51%. By 2007, more than 97% of everything communicated over telecom networks traveled over the internet, leaving traditional phone lines and telegrams as an afterthought.

Speeds kept climbing too, with fiber-optic networks reaching 800 gigabits per second by 2019, letting more people stream, upload, and connect than ever before. Growth shows no sign of stopping, driven by ever more online information, commerce, entertainment, and social networking, though regional differences may still shape how the network's future unfolds.

8 key facts

  1. In 1993 the internet carried just 1% of the world's two-way telecommunications traffic; by 2000 that share had jumped to 51%.
  2. By 2007, more than 97% of the world's telecommunicated information traveled over the internet.
  3. Paul Baran and Donald Davies invented the idea of packet switching independently of each other, on opposite sides of the Atlantic, in the early-to-mid 1960s.
  4. ARPANET's first computers were connected through machines called Interface Message Processors, built by a team at Bolt, Beranek, and Newman.
  5. The core language of the internet, TCP/IP, was described in a 1974 research paper by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn.
  6. Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web at CERN in Switzerland in 1989-90, two decades after the internet itself began.
  7. The NSFNET's optical backbone was only decommissioned in 1995, the moment that removed the last restrictions on commercial internet traffic in the United States.
  8. By 2019, fiber-optic networks were carrying data at speeds up to 800 gigabits per second.

Key terms, decoded

ARPANET
The pioneering US computer network built starting in 1969 that connected research institutions and is considered the internet's direct ancestor.
Domain Name System
A system that translates easy-to-remember website names into the numeric addresses computers actually use to find each other.
Ethernet
A common technology for connecting computers together over a wired local network.
fiber-optic cable
A cable made of thin glass strands that carries data as pulses of light instead of electricity, allowing much faster transmission.
Interface Message Processor
An early networking computer that connected a university or research site to the ARPANET, acting like a specialized router.
internet service provider
A company that sells people and businesses a connection to the internet.
NSFNET
A US government-funded computer network from the 1980s that connected university supercomputing centers and became a major backbone of the early internet.
packet switching
A way of sending data by breaking a message into small chunks, called packets, sending each one separately across a network, and reassembling them at the destination.
TCP/IP
A pair of rules, called Transmission Control Protocol and Internet Protocol, that control how data is broken up, addressed, and delivered between computers on the internet.
wave division multiplexing
A technology that sends many separate streams of data down the same fiber-optic cable at once by using different colors of light.
World Wide Web
A system of linked documents, called web pages, that anyone can access and jump between using a connected computer.
X.25
An early standard that let different computer networks exchange data reliably over long-distance phone lines.
