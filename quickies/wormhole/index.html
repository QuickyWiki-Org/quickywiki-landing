Wormhole

A wormhole could get you somewhere faster than a light beam traveling outside it. Yet a light beam sent through the same tunnel would still beat you.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 8 terms decoded

A Tunnel With Two Ends

Picture a tunnel whose two ends open in different places, different times, or both. That is a wormhole, a shortcut through spacetime. Wormholes are still hypothetical. But they come from a real solution to Einstein's equations, and they fit within the theory of general relativity.

The name has a playful history. In 1928, the mathematician Hermann Weyl wrote about the idea but called the shapes "one-dimensional tubes". That same year, the astronomer Arthur Eddington used the word "worm-holes" in a popular book, but for something else entirely.

The word as we use it came from the physicist John Archibald Wheeler. In a 1957 paper with Charles Misner, he noted that mathematicians would call the shape "a handle". Physicists, he wrote, "might perhaps be excused for more vividly terming" it a "wormhole".

The Bridge Nobody Can Cross

The first wormhole in the math was found by Ludwig Flamm in 1916, a few months after Schwarzschild published his famous solution describing a black hole. Albert Einstein and Nathan Rosen found it again and published it in 1935. Today it is called the Einstein-Rosen bridge.

It hides in the math of an idealized black hole that has existed forever. Follow that math all the way and you find a black hole, a white hole that particles rise out of, and two separate outside regions, sometimes called two different "universes". Drawn at a single moment, the shape looks like a tube joining them.

Sadly for travelers, this bridge is useless. In 1962, Wheeler and Robert Fuller showed that it pinches shut so fast that not even light can make it from one side to the other. And a real black hole formed from a collapsing star would not have this bridge at all.

Holding the Throat Open

So could anyone build a wormhole you could actually travel through? Homer Ellis and K. A. Bronnikov each showed in 1973 that such crossable wormholes were possible in theory. In 1988, Kip Thorne and his student Mike Morris described one held open by a shell of exotic matter. They even suggested using it as a tool to teach general relativity.

Exotic matter carries negative energy, which sounds like fantasy. But the Casimir effect shows that the energy in certain regions of space can dip below that of empty space. Early calculations suggested a wormhole would need a huge amount of negative energy. Later ones showed it could be made as small as you like.

Nature might make wormholes too. Tiny ones could pop in and out of existence at the smallest scales, and stable ones have been suggested as candidates for dark matter. A tiny wormhole born near the Big Bang might even have been stretched to a huge size.

Beating Light Without Going Faster

Nothing can move faster than light, but that rule only applies locally. A wormhole could bend the rule without breaking it. If the tunnel is shorter than the normal route, a traveler moving slower than light could arrive before a light beam sent the long way. The funny part is that a light beam sent through the same wormhole would still beat the traveler.

Wormholes might even work as time machines. One idea is to speed one mouth up to near light speed and then bring it back. Thanks to time dilation, that end ages less and becomes "younger". Step into the younger end, and you come out of the older end in the past, as seen from outside.

There is a catch. You could never go back to a time before the machine was made. And in 1993, Matt Visser argued that the wormhole would collapse, or its mouths would push apart, before it could break cause and effect.

Parallel Worlds and Science Fiction

Time travel creates paradoxes, and one proposed way out involves other universes. In 1991, David Deutsch showed that quantum theory stays consistent even in spacetimes with a closed timelike curve, a loop in time. In one view, a particle returning from the future does not land back in its own universe but in a parallel universe. A wormhole time machine with a tiny time jump would then be a bridge between universes existing side by side.

Science fiction loves wormholes for a simple reason. They let characters cross between stars, galaxies and even universes within a human lifetime. Some stories use them for time travel too.

Stories also borrow the warp portal, a shortcut that folds space through a hidden fourth dimension. Think of a flat sheet. To bring two distant points together, you must bend it through a third dimension. In the same way, our space would need a fourth one to fold.

10 key facts

  1. Wormholes come from a special solution of Einstein's field equations and fit within general relativity.
  2. In 1928, Hermann Weyl described the idea as "one-dimensional tubes" without using the word wormhole.
  3. John Archibald Wheeler used the term wormhole in a 1957 paper written with Charles Misner.
  4. Ludwig Flamm found the Einstein-Rosen bridge in 1916, 19 years before Einstein and Rosen published it.
  5. In 1962, Wheeler and Robert Fuller showed that an Einstein-Rosen bridge pinches off too fast even for light to cross.
  6. Kip Thorne and Mike Morris argued in 1988 that a wormhole could be used as a tool for teaching general relativity.
  7. Later calculations showed that the negative energy needed for a wormhole could be made arbitrarily small.
  8. A light beam traveling through a wormhole would still beat a traveler going through the same wormhole.
  9. A wormhole time machine could never take you back to a time before it was created.
  10. Stable tiny wormholes have been suggested as candidates for dark matter.

Key terms, decoded

closed timelike curve
A path through spacetime that loops back to its own starting point in time.
exotic matter
Hypothetical material with negative energy or negative mass, which could hold a wormhole open.
general relativity
The theory that describes gravity as the bending of spacetime by mass and energy.
negative energy
Energy lower than that of empty space. Small amounts can appear in special quantum situations.
parallel universe
A separate universe that exists alongside our own.
spacetime
Space and time joined into one fabric. Physicists treat the three dimensions of space and one of time together.
time dilation
The effect where time passes more slowly for something moving very fast or sitting in strong gravity.
white hole
The reverse of a black hole in the math: a region that things can only come out of, never fall into.
