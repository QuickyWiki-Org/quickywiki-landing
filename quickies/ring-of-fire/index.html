Ring of Fire

A single 40,000 km belt wrapped around the Pacific holds about two-thirds of the world's volcanoes and sets off about 90% of its earthquakes.

4 min read · 10 key facts · 4 terms decoded

A Belt of 900 Volcanoes Around One Ocean

Picture a chain about 40,000 km long and up to about 500 km wide, wrapped around most of the Pacific Ocean. That is the Ring of Fire. It holds between 750 and 915 active or dormant volcanoes, around two-thirds of the world total. The exact count depends on which regions you include.

It is also the planet's busiest earthquake zone. About 90% of the world's earthquakes happen here, including most of the largest ones. The next busiest region, the Alpide belt, gets only about 5-6%.

Here is the twist: the Ring of Fire is not one single structure. It is many separate plate boundaries that happen to circle the same ocean. It has existed for more than 35 million years, and more than 350 of its volcanoes have been active in historical times.

Why a Volcano Belt Is Named After Fire

The name is a leftover from an old idea. From Ancient Greek and Roman times until the late 18th century, people believed volcanoes were caused by fires burning inside the Earth. Volcanoes do not actually burn anything with fire, but the name stuck.

Scientists noticed the pattern long before they could explain it. In 1825, the volcano expert G. P. Scrope described chains of volcanoes around the Pacific rim. A book about the Perry Expedition to Japan in the 1850s spoke of an "immense circle" of volcanoes around the Pacific. A 1906 book about the San Francisco earthquake used the phrase "the great ring of fire".

In 1912, geologist Patrick Marshall drew the "Andesite Line" in the southwest Pacific, which closely matches the Ring. The full explanation only arrived with the theory of plate tectonics in the early 1960s.

Plates Diving Under Plates

The Ring of Fire exists because of subduction. Around the Pacific, ocean plates are being pushed down beneath their neighbours. The Nazca and Cocos plates slide under South America. The Pacific plate dives beneath the Aleutian Islands and Kamchatka. Where they sink, the seafloor forms deep oceanic trenches, including the Mariana Trench.

What grows on top depends on what is above. When an ocean plate sinks under another ocean plate, a curved chain of volcanic islands forms, like the Mariana Arc. When it sinks under a continent, a line of volcanoes rises along the coast, like in Chile.

The angle matters too. Older seafloor dives more steeply. The Pacific's ridges, where new seafloor is made, sit much closer to South America than to Asia. So young seafloor slides under South America at a shallow angle, while old seafloor plunges steeply in the western Pacific.

The Ring Has Holes in It

Despite its name, the ring is not complete. In parts of the south Pacific, between New Zealand and Antarctica, the plates pull apart instead of colliding, so there is no subduction belt there. There are also gaps along the Americas, such as in northern Mexico and southern California, partly because of the San Andreas Fault.

Some famous volcanoes are not part of it at all. Hawaii sits in the middle of the Pacific, far from any subduction zone, so it does not count. Australia is left out too, because it lies in the centre of its own plate.

Geologists even argue about the edges. Indonesia sits where the Ring meets the Alpide belt. Many experts place its western islands, home to Krakatoa and Toba, in the Alpide belt instead. The Antarctic Peninsula and some Pacific island chains are also disputed.

Record Quakes, Lava Lakes and Rich Soil

The Ring holds some huge records. The four largest volcanic eruptions of the last 11,700 years all happened here, and so did twenty of the twenty-five largest. The largest earthquake ever recorded, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, measured 9.4-9.6.

Some stories sound unreal. Villarrica in Chile is one of only five volcanoes in the world known to have an active lava lake in its crater. In 1993, Lascar in Chile dropped ash on Buenos Aires, more than 1,600 km away. Japan holds about 10% of the world's active volcanoes and records as many as 1,500 earthquakes a year.

There is a bright side underground. Weathered volcanic ash forms soils called andosols, which are full of volcanic glass. The Ring of Fire is the world's main home for this soil, and it is usually quite fertile.

10 key facts

  1. The Ring of Fire is about 40,000 km long and up to about 500 km wide.
  2. It contains between 750 and 915 active or dormant volcanoes, around two-thirds of the world total.
  3. About 90% of the world's earthquakes happen along the Ring of Fire.
  4. Hawaii is not part of the Ring of Fire because it is far from any subduction zone.
  5. The four largest volcanic eruptions of the last 11,700 years all happened at Ring of Fire volcanoes.
  6. A huge Cascadia earthquake in 1700 is known partly through Japanese records, because its waves crossed the whole Pacific.
  7. Villarrica in Chile is one of only five volcanoes worldwide known to have an active lava lake.
  8. The 1993 eruption of Lascar in Chile dropped ash on Buenos Aires, more than 1,600 km away.
  9. The Cascade volcanic arc in the western United States has over 4,000 separate volcanic vents.
  10. The name comes from an old belief, held until the late 18th century, that volcanoes were fires burning inside the Earth.

Key terms, decoded

andosols
Soils formed from weathered volcanic ash. They contain lots of volcanic glass and are usually fertile.
oceanic trenches
Long, deep cuts in the ocean floor that form where one plate sinks under another.
plate tectonics
The theory that Earth's outer shell is broken into large moving plates. Their movements explain where volcanoes and earthquakes happen.
subduction
The process where one tectonic plate is pushed down beneath another plate into the Earth.
