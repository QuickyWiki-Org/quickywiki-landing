Ignaz Semmelweis

He cut deaths on his maternity ward from 18% to under 2% by asking doctors to wash their hands. They were offended, mocked him, and had him committed to an asylum, where he died.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 11 terms decoded

The Ward Where Doctors Killed More Than Midwives

Ignaz Philipp Semmelweis (1 July 1818 – 13 August 1865) was a Hungarian doctor and scientist of German descent, an early pioneer of antiseptic procedures, known as the "saviour of mothers".

The problem he faced was routine and lethal. Postpartum infection — also called puerperal fever or childbed fever — was common in the 19th century and often fatal. Women survived childbirth and then died days later.

At Vienna General Hospital's First Obstetrical Clinic, Semmelweis noticed something that should have been impossible to ignore: the doctors' wards had three times the mortality of the midwives' wards. Same hospital, same city, same women. The difference was who attended the birth — and doctors, unlike midwives, moved between autopsies and deliveries.

Eighteen Percent, Down to Two

In 1847 Semmelweis proposed something almost insultingly simple: hand washing with chlorinated lime solutions.

The result was immediate and enormous. The maternal mortality rate dropped from 18% to less than 2%.

Sit with those numbers. Nearly one in five women dying, reduced to fewer than one in fifty, by a basin of disinfectant. He had demonstrated that the incidence of infection could be drastically reduced simply by requiring healthcare workers in obstetrical clinics to disinfect their hands, and in 1861 he published his findings as a book, *Etiology, Concept and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever*. He had the data, he had the intervention, and he had the corpses that stopped appearing.

Rejected for Being Right Too Early

The medical community rejected it. Semmelweis's observations conflicted with the established scientific views of the time, and there was a genuine intellectual gap in his case: he could not offer a theoretical explanation for why hand washing reduced mortality. He knew *that* it worked, not *why*.

The other objection was not intellectual at all. Some doctors were offended by the suggestion that they should wash their hands — that a gentleman physician might be carrying death from one patient to the next — and they mocked him for it.

So the argument was lost on two fronts simultaneously: an incomplete theory, and wounded professional pride. The evidence was overwhelming and it did not matter. Women continued to die in wards where the intervention was known, available, and refused.

Committed, Beaten, and Vindicated Too Late

The ending is brutal. In 1865, the increasingly outspoken Semmelweis purportedly suffered a nervous breakdown and was committed to an asylum by his own colleagues.

In the asylum he was beaten by the guards. He died 14 days later, aged 47, from a gangrenous wound on his right hand that may have been caused by the beating — killed, in all likelihood, by exactly the kind of infection he had spent his career trying to prevent.

Vindication came only after his death. Louis Pasteur confirmed the germ theory of disease, which finally supplied the theoretical explanation Semmelweis had lacked, and Joseph Lister, acting on Pasteur's research, pioneered antiseptic surgical methods with great success. The man had been right the entire time. He simply could not prove why, and the profession punished him for the gap.

Key terms, decoded

antiseptic
Describing a substance or method that kills germs and prevents infection.
chlorinated lime
A chlorine-based powder mixed with water to make a strong disinfectant solution.
etiology
The study of the causes of a disease.
gangrenous
Describing tissue that has died and begun to decay, usually from lost blood supply or severe infection.
germ theory
The understanding that many diseases are caused by microscopic organisms that spread from person to person.
maternal mortality rate
The proportion of women who die from causes related to pregnancy and childbirth.
midwife
A trained professional who assists women through pregnancy and delivery.
obstetrical clinic
A hospital department that cares for women during pregnancy and childbirth.
postpartum infection
An infection that develops in a woman after she has given birth.
prophylaxis
Action taken to prevent a disease rather than to treat it once it appears.
puerperal fever
A once-common and often fatal infection of the womb after childbirth, also called childbed fever.
