Null Island

It has a flag, a fake history, and a cult following online — but Null Island isn't a real island at all, just an empty patch of ocean where two invisible lines cross.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 6 terms decoded

A Joke That Grew Its Own Flag

In 2008, a mapmaker named Steve Pellegrin invented an island that doesn't exist. He called it Null Island and built a website for the Republic of Null Island, with the tagline "Like no place on Earth." It was a private joke among mapping nerds, but the internet ran with it.

People gave Null Island a flag, a fake geography, and an invented history, even though it's really just open ocean. Its imagined look often echoes the eerie floating islands from the video game Myst, and fans link it to Captain Nemo's hideaway, Lincoln Island, from Jules Verne's novels.

The joke wasn't even the first time someone dreamed up land at these coordinates. A 1957 cartoon called Colonel Bleep placed a "Zero Zero Island" there, and a 1969 film, Latitude Zero, imagined a hidden utopia on the seafloor at the exact same spot.

Where Bad Data Washes Ashore

Computers hate empty answers. When mapping software can't figure out where something is, it often fills in the blank with zero for both latitude and longitude, and that lands exactly on Null Island. That's the real reason this empty patch of ocean matters: it's a magnet for mistakes.

The mapping company Natural Earth added Null Island to its free public map data around 2010, describing it as a "1 meter square island" that should never actually appear on a real map. It exists purely as a flag for broken location records.

In January 2018, the investigative group Bellingcat found something stranger. People using the fitness-tracking app Strava had activity logged at Null Island, not by accident, but because they had deliberately entered fake coordinates there to hide where they really were.

The Emptiest Spot on the Map

Strip away the jokes, and Null Island is a real spot with real coordinates: zero degrees latitude, zero degrees longitude, sitting in international waters in the Atlantic Ocean near the Gulf of Guinea. The water here runs deep, about 4,940 meters, or roughly 16,200 feet, straight down to the seabed.

There's no island, no sandbar, nothing above the waves. The closest scrap of land is about 570 kilometers north, near a point on Ghana's coastline called Achowa Point, between the towns of Akwidaa and Dixcove.

So the name is pure irony. An "island" that is, in fact, the middle of nowhere: open ocean, hundreds of kilometers from the nearest shore in any direction.

The Buoy Named Soul

For years, Null Island wasn't entirely empty. A weather buoy called Soul floated right at zero-zero, one of 17 ocean-monitoring buoys placed there since 1997 by the United States, France, and Brazil under a program called PIRATA. In a whimsical touch, every buoy in the network was named after a music genre.

Soul's design was simple: a 3.8-meter, cone-shaped float anchored to the seabed by cable, built to track ocean and weather data far from any coastline. It didn't last long at its post. The buoy disappeared less than a year after installation and had to be replaced in 1998.

The program kept watch over that spot for over two decades before operations there finally ended in March 2021.

10 key facts

  1. Null Island sits exactly where the prime meridian meets the equator, at zero degrees latitude and zero degrees longitude in the Atlantic Ocean.
  2. Despite its name, there is no actual island there, just open water about 4,940 meters deep.
  3. The nearest land is roughly 570 kilometers away, near Achowa Point on Ghana's coastline.
  4. Mapping software often assigns coordinates of 0,0 to locations it cannot identify, which is why Null Island became a magnet for data errors.
  5. In 2018, the investigative group Bellingcat found that Strava fitness app users had faked their location as Null Island to hide where they really were.
  6. A real weather buoy named Soul once floated at these exact coordinates, part of a 17-buoy ocean-monitoring network called PIRATA.
  7. Every buoy in the PIRATA network was named after a music genre.
  8. The Soul buoy vanished less than a year after it was installed in 1997 and had to be replaced in 1998.
  9. The name Null Island began as a joke started by mapmaker Steve Pellegrin in 2008, complete with a fake Republic of Null Island website.
  10. Decades before that internet joke, a 1957 cartoon and a 1969 film both imagined a fictional island sitting at these same zero coordinates.

Key terms, decoded

buoy
A floating device anchored in water that carries instruments to measure things like weather, waves, or ocean temperature.
coordinates
A pair of numbers, like latitude and longitude, that pinpoint an exact location on a map or globe.
equator
An imaginary line circling the middle of the Earth at zero degrees latitude, splitting the planet into a northern half and a southern half.
international waters
Ocean areas that don't belong to any single country and are open for ships and research from around the world.
PIRATA
A joint ocean-monitoring program run by the United States, France, and Brazil that uses a network of floating buoys to track weather and sea conditions in the Atlantic.
prime meridian
An imaginary line running from the North Pole to the South Pole that marks zero degrees longitude, the starting point mapmakers use to measure east-west position.
