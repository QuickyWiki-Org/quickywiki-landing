Voyager Golden Record

In 1977, a small team with six weeks and $1,500 pressed humanity's greatest hits onto two golden disks — then hurled them out of the Solar System for aliens to find.

3 min read · 9 key facts · 4 terms decoded

A Mixtape for Aliens on a Shoestring Budget

When NASA prepared the two Voyager spacecraft for launch in 1977, project manager John Casani handed astronomer Carl Sagan a strange side quest: put a message aboard for anyone — or anything — that might one day find the probes. Sagan teamed up with Linda Salzman and Frank Drake, with a budget of just $1,500 for six weeks of work. They ended up dipping into their own pockets.

The team chose a copper phonograph record over magnetic tape because it would better survive the harshness of space. Deciding what belonged on it was harder — choosing the sounds, images, and music that would speak for an entire planet took nearly a year.

What Humanity Chose to Say

The record carries 116 images, from diagrams of DNA and the Solar System to photographs of landscapes, animals, food, and daily human life. Its audio side holds greetings in 55 languages, the sounds of surf, wind, thunder, birds, and whales, and a message from a six-year-old — Sagan's son Nick.

The music sparked the best arguments. Alongside Bach, Beethoven, and Mozart sit Bulgarian folk songs, Indian classical music, and Chuck Berry's Johnny B. Goode — included over the objection of an ethnomusicologist who called rock music "adolescent". Sagan's reply: "there are a lot of adolescents on the planet."

The Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun" almost made it, but record label EMI reportedly wanted $50,000 — when the whole production cost $18,000. Strangest of all: a one-minute recording of creative director Ann Druyan's brainwaves, captured while she thought about Earth's history and what it feels like to fall in love.

Built to Outlive Everything We Know

Each record is a copper disk plated with nickel and gold, 12 inches across, meant to be played at 16⅔ revolutions per minute. Its aluminum cover is electroplated with uranium-238 — a deliberate clock. Because uranium-238 decays with a half-life of 4.468 billion years, any finder can measure what remains and calculate how long the record has been traveling.

The cover also carries playback instructions written in binary, using the fundamental frequency of the hydrogen atom as a universal unit of time. One human touch nearly didn't survive: the hand-etched inscription "To the makers of music – all worlds, all times" wasn't in the specifications, and the record was initially rejected because of it. Sagan had to convince administrators to let it fly.

Where the Bottle Is Now

Voyager 1 crossed into interstellar space on September 12, 2013, carrying its record with it. Neither probe is aimed at any particular star, but in about 40,000 years Voyager 1 will drift within 1.6 light-years of the star Gliese 445, while Voyager 2 approaches Ross 248.

Back home, the record refuses to be forgotten. NASA uploaded its sounds to SoundCloud in 2015, and a 2017 Kickstarter for a 40th-anniversary vinyl edition raised over $1.4 million — winning a Grammy Award in 2018. Sagan said launching this "bottle" into the "cosmic ocean" says something hopeful about life on Earth. The message may never be found. It was still worth sending.

9 key facts

  1. The team behind the Golden Record had a budget of just $1,500 for six weeks of work and supplemented it with personal funds.
  2. The record holds 116 images and greetings in 55 ancient and modern languages.
  3. The Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun" was left off because EMI reportedly demanded $50,000 per record, while the entire production cost $18,000.
  4. A one-minute compressed recording of Ann Druyan's brainwaves is on the record, captured while she contemplated Earth's history and love.
  5. The record's cover is electroplated with uranium-238 so any finder can date it from radioactive decay across billions of years.
  6. Sound engineer Jimmy Iovine joined the project on the recommendation of John Lennon, who was contacted but unable to participate.
  7. The hand-etched inscription "To the makers of music – all worlds, all times" initially caused the record to be rejected by quality control.
  8. In about 40,000 years, Voyager 1 will pass within 1.6 light-years of the star Gliese 445.
  9. A 40th-anniversary vinyl reissue raised over $1.4 million on Kickstarter and won a Grammy Award in 2018.

Key terms, decoded

binary
A way of writing information using only two symbols, usually 0 and 1. It is the simplest counting system a technological civilization could recognize.
half-life
The time it takes for half of a radioactive material to decay. Measuring how much remains reveals how much time has passed.
interstellar space
The region between stars, beyond the influence of our Sun's wind of particles.
phonograph record
A flat disk with a spiral groove that stores sound. A needle riding the groove vibrates and reproduces the recorded audio.
