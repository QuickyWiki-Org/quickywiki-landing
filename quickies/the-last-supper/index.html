The Last Supper

Leonardo painted it with materials that let him revise as he worked — and that is exactly why almost none of the original survives today.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 12 terms decoded

A Painting on a Dining Room Wall

The Last Supper — *Il Cenacolo* or *L'Ultima Cena* in Italian — is a mural painting by the Italian High Renaissance artist Leonardo da Vinci, dated to c. 1495–1498.

It is not in a gallery and never has been. It is housed in the refectory — the dining hall — of the Convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan. The monks ate their meals in front of it.

The work depicts the scene of the Last Supper as told in the Gospel of John, and specifically one instant within it: the moment after the announcement that one of those present will betray him. Choosing that particular second is what the painting is built around — it is a picture of a room reacting. Its handling of space, mastery of perspective, treatment of motion and complex display of human emotion have made it one of the Western world's most recognisable paintings.

Commissioned by a Duke

The work was commissioned as part of a plan of renovations to the church and its convent buildings by Leonardo's patron Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan.

Some commentators consider it pivotal in inaugurating the transition into what is now termed the High Renaissance — the short period in which Italian art reached the balance, scale and confidence that later centuries treated as a standard.

It is also, aside from the Sala delle Asse, Leonardo's largest work. That combination — his biggest surviving painting, made at a hinge point in the history of European art, executed on a wall in a dining room for the monks of one convent — is a reminder of how much major Renaissance art was produced as architectural decoration for a specific room rather than as a portable object.

The Technique That Doomed It

Here is the decision that determines everything about the painting's condition today.

Leonardo worked slowly, with an inconsistent painting schedule and frequent revisions. Traditional fresco does not permit that: pigment is applied into wet plaster and must be finished before it dries, with no going back.

So he used materials that allowed for regular alterations instead — tempera on gesso, pitch and mastic. It gave him the freedom to keep changing his mind, and it gave the wall no durability whatsoever.

The result was deterioration that began almost immediately. Due to the methods used, a variety of environmental factors, and intentional damage, little of the original painting remains today despite numerous restoration attempts, the last of which was completed in 1999. The most famous mural in the world is largely a record of what was there.

Why It Still Works

Given how little original paint survives, the painting's grip on people needs explaining — and it comes down to structure rather than surface.

The composition organises twelve figures into four groups of three, each reacting differently to the same sentence, so the eye reads a sequence of responses rather than a row of portraits. The room's perspective lines converge on the central figure, meaning the architecture itself directs attention before any detail does.

That design survives damage, restoration and reproduction. It is legible in a faded wall, a printed copy, or a photograph on a phone — which is precisely why the image became inescapable, and why commentators still credit its handling of space and motion as the reason it marked a turning point in Western painting.

10 key facts

  1. The Last Supper is a mural in the dining hall of the Convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan.
  2. It was painted around 1495 to 1498 by Leonardo da Vinci.
  3. It depicts the moment immediately after Jesus announces that one of the apostles will betray him, as told in the Gospel of John.
  4. It was commissioned by Leonardo's patron Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, as part of renovations to the convent.
  5. Leonardo avoided traditional fresco because it must be finished before the plaster dries, allowing no revision.
  6. He used tempera on gesso, pitch and mastic instead, so he could keep altering the work.
  7. Those materials are the main reason little of the original painting survives today.
  8. Environmental factors and intentional damage also contributed to its deterioration.
  9. The most recent restoration was completed in 1999.
  10. Aside from the Sala delle Asse, it is Leonardo's largest work.

Key terms, decoded

composition
The arrangement of the elements within a picture.
convent
A building housing a religious community.
fresco
A wall-painting method in which pigment is applied into wet plaster and becomes part of the wall as it dries.
gesso
A white chalky ground layer applied to a surface before painting.
High Renaissance
The peak phase of Italian Renaissance art, around the late 1400s and early 1500s.
mastic
A tree resin used as a varnish or binder in painting.
mural
A painting made directly onto a wall rather than on a movable panel or canvas.
patron
Someone who commissions and pays for an artist's work.
perspective
The technique of representing three-dimensional depth on a flat surface so objects recede convincingly.
refectory
The communal dining hall of a monastery or convent.
restoration
Conservation work that stabilises and repairs a damaged artwork.
tempera
Paint made by binding pigment with a substance such as egg, which dries fast and hard.
