Yawn

Reading about yawning will probably make you yawn — a contagion so strong it jumps from humans to dogs, and science still can't fully explain why we do it.

3 min read · 10 key facts · 9 terms decoded

Six Seconds of Total Takeover

A yawn is a reflex in three acts: a long, slow inhale as the mouth gapes open, a brief climax with muscles stretched taut, and a quick exhale as everything relaxes. The whole performance usually lasts about six seconds — and while it runs, it owns you. The airway dilates to three or four times its normal size.

There's even a built-in sound effect. The tensor tympani muscle in your middle ear contracts during a yawn, producing that low internal rumble you hear from inside your own head — mechanical disturbance of the hearing apparatus, not moving air. And this is no human quirk: nearly all vertebrates yawn — mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, even fish. The study of yawning has its own name: chasmology.

The Most Contagious Thing You Do

See a yawn, hear a yawn, read the word yawn — and the urge arrives. Contagious yawning is real and remarkably robust: it can be triggered just by talking on the phone with someone who yawns. Stranger still, it leaps across species. Chimpanzees, dogs, cats, birds, and reptiles all catch yawns, sometimes from us.

In a University of London study, 21 of 29 dogs yawned when a stranger yawned in front of them — but not when the stranger merely opened his mouth. Puppies under seven months proved immune, and human children generally don't catch yawns before about age six, hinting the contagion is something we grow into. As early as 1508, Erasmus wrote that one man's yawning makes another yawn. Five centuries later, science agrees — and still argues about why.

An Accidental Empathy Meter?

One seductive theory holds that catching yawns reflects empathy — unconsciously mirroring another's state. The evidence is intriguing: a 2011 University of Pisa study found yawn contagion was best predicted by social bonding, spreading most readily between kin, then friends, then acquaintances, and least between strangers. A 2007 study found that children with autism spectrum disorder did not increase their yawning after watching videos of others yawning, unlike non-autistic children.

But the literature is genuinely contested. Substantial disagreement persists over whether contagious yawning truly tracks empathy, and publication bias — the tendency for positive findings to get published while null results vanish — may inflate the connection. The honest summary: the world's most shareable behavior remains only half-explained.

Nobody Knows Why You Just Did That

Scholars have proposed roughly twenty explanations for yawning, with little agreement on any. The famous one — that yawning tops up low oxygen — has been debunked: yawning frequency doesn't drop when people breathe extra oxygen. A 2007 hypothesis reframes the yawn as a brain-cooling mechanism; people with cold packs on their foreheads caught fewer yawns. Another camp points to arousal — paratroopers famously yawn moments before jumping out of aircraft, priming for action rather than dozing off.

Whatever its purpose, yawning starts astonishingly early — fetuses yawn in the womb by 30 weeks — and it moonlights across the animal kingdom in unexpected roles: baboons yawn to flash their canine teeth at rivals, guinea pigs yawn to display dominance, penguins yawn as part of courtship, and fish yawn more when oxygen runs low. George Washington's advice still applies, though: if you must yawn, do it behind your hand.

10 key facts

  1. A typical yawn lasts about six seconds and dilates the airway to three or four times its normal size.
  2. The rumbling sound you hear while yawning comes from a muscle contracting in your own middle ear.
  3. In one study, 21 of 29 dogs caught yawns from a yawning stranger, but puppies under seven months were immune.
  4. Children generally do not catch yawns before about age six.
  5. The theory that yawning boosts oxygen intake has been debunked experimentally.
  6. People with cold packs on their foreheads catch fewer yawns, supporting the brain-cooling hypothesis.
  7. Paratroopers often yawn moments before jumping out of aircraft.
  8. Fetuses yawn in the womb by 30 weeks of pregnancy.
  9. Baboons yawn to threaten rivals by displaying their canine teeth.
  10. Erasmus noted in 1508 that one person's yawn makes another yawn.

Key terms, decoded

arousal
The body's state of alertness and readiness for action.
autism spectrum disorder
A developmental condition affecting social communication and behavior.
brain-cooling hypothesis
The proposal that yawning helps lower the brain's temperature.
chasmology
The scientific study of yawning.
empathy
The capacity to share and understand another's feelings and states.
pandiculation
The combined act of yawning while stretching the whole body.
publication bias
The distortion that arises because striking positive results get published more often than null findings.
reflex
An automatic body response that happens without conscious decision.
tensor tympani
A small middle-ear muscle whose contraction during a yawn causes the internal rumbling sound.
